# **Rock Generator Lite: Documentation**

## **1\. Introduction**

Rock Generator Lite is the free entry version of the Veridian Rock Generator toolset for Unity. It lets you create procedural rock meshes, preview them in the Editor, bake materials, generate reusable prefabs, and scatter those prefabs across a Unity Terrain.

Lite is usable on its own, especially for learning the workflow, creating individual rocks, testing presets, or building smaller rock sets. It is also intended to give you a clear hands-on preview of the Rock Generator workflow before moving to **Rock Generator Pro**.

If you plan to use this tool seriously in a production environment, **Rock Generator Pro is the recommended version**. Pro can do the Lite workflow, but adds the features that matter more as a project grows: batch rock generation, a stronger placement workflow, local brush placement, higher texture bake resolutions, more noise methods, more texturing methods, more texture inputs, runtime MonoBehaviour tools, and a material/texture atlas combiner for rocks and other assets.

Rock Generator Lite is the free version. Rock Generator Pro is the fuller production workflow.

This documentation covers **Rock Generator Lite only**. Rock Generator Pro uses a different codebase and has its own documentation.

---

### **What Rock Generator Lite Does**

Rock Generator Lite creates rocks from procedural settings rather than from imported meshes. You choose a base shape, adjust shape and noise settings, preview the result, choose a texturing method, then save the generated rock as a normal Unity prefab.

A typical Lite workflow looks like this:

1. Open the Rock Window.  
2. Choose a Quick Start Preset or create a new rock profile.  
3. Adjust the seed, size, proportions, shape, noise, color, texture, LOD, and collider settings.  
4. Preview the rock in the built-in viewport.  
5. Generate a prefab into your Unity project.  
6. Optionally use the Rock Placer to scatter generated prefabs on a Unity Terrain.

The generator is not a geological simulation. It is a game-art tool that uses procedural geometry, noise, color patterns, baked normals, cavity shading, metallic overlays, and related tricks to create rocks that read well in a scene. Some presets are grounded in recognizable stone types, such as sandstone, basalt, granite, shale, pumice, limestone, quartz, or marble. Others are more stylized, exaggerated, fantasy-oriented, or designed to make interesting mineral effects more obvious for game use.

That distinction matters. For example, a gold-veined or pyrite-style rock may use stronger, clearer metallic bands than would usually appear in a natural boulder at that scale. That is intentional. In games, readability often matters more than strict geology.

---

### **A Preset-First Workflow**

Rock Generator Lite includes a set of Quick Start Presets. These are the recommended starting point for most users.

The controls in Rock Generator Lite are layered. Shape noise affects the mesh. Texturing noise affects color patterns. Bump and micro-detail affect the baked normal map. Metallic settings affect albedo, metallic, and smoothness outputs. Slope, cavity, strata, patch, warp, and Voronoi settings can all interact.

Because of that, randomly sliding every control is not usually the best way to make good rocks. It can produce noisy surfaces, strange color placement, over-sharp bump maps, plastic-looking smoothness, or rocks that look more like procedural test objects than natural stone.

A better workflow is:

1. Start from a preset that is close to the type of rock you want.  
2. Change the seed to find a better variation.  
3. Adjust proportions or prefab scale.  
4. Tune the large shape before the small detail.  
5. Tune colors before bump and micro-detail.  
6. Use texture baking when the rock already reads well in shape and color.

The presets are not meant to be final locked templates. They are learning examples and practical starting points. Apply one, inspect what it changes, then adjust a small number of settings at a time.

---

### **Generated Output Assets**

Generated rocks are your generated output assets.

You may use, modify, sell, and redistribute generated rock meshes, materials, textures, and prefabs in games, demos, scenes, rendered media, asset packs, commercial projects, and other creative work.

This permission applies to the generated output assets only. It does **not** give permission to redistribute the Rock Generator Lite or Rock Generator Pro package itself, source code, editor tools, compute shaders, documentation, demo scripts, original package files, or other tool files included with the asset.

In practical terms:

* A rock prefab you generate is your output asset.  
* A mesh saved by the generator is your output asset.  
* A baked albedo, normal, mask, AO, height, smoothness, or metallic texture generated by the tool is your output asset.  
* The generator scripts, Editor windows, compute shader, demo tools, and package files are still part of the Rock Generator asset package.

This is one of the main advantages of the baked prefab workflow. After generation, the rock behaves like a normal Unity asset that can be organized, duplicated, placed, scattered, or included in builds.

---

### **Clean Prefab Output**

Generated baked rocks are saved as normal Unity prefabs.

Depending on the selected settings, a generated prefab can include:

* A root GameObject.  
* Child objects for visual LODs.  
* MeshFilter and MeshRenderer components.  
* A Unity LODGroup.  
* Generated mesh assets.  
* A generated material.  
* Baked texture maps.  
* Optional primitive, convex mesh, or exact mesh collision.

For baked texture output, Rock Generator Lite uses standard Unity material paths where available, including URP Lit, HDRP Lit, or the Built-in Standard shader fallback. If your project uses a custom shader, you may want to adjust the generated material after export.

Generated baked prefabs do not require Rock Generator Lite Editor MonoBehaviours to function after export. The generator is used to create the prefab; the prefab itself is then a normal Unity asset.

Vertex Color output is different. In Vertex Color mode, procedural color is stored in the mesh vertex colors instead of baked texture files. This can be useful for lightweight or mobile-style assets, but the material or shader must read vertex colors correctly. If the shader does not display vertex colors, the rock may appear uncolored or incorrectly colored.

---

### **Lite and Pro**

Rock Generator Lite includes the core workflow for making individual procedural rocks. It is best understood as the entry version of the tool. It is useful for testing the generator, learning the controls, making smaller rock sets, and deciding whether the full Pro workflow fits your project.

Rock Generator Pro is the main version of the asset. It is intended for broader, heavier, and more production-oriented use.

Compared with Lite, Pro adds:

* Batch rock generation.  
* A more sophisticated Rock Placer.  
* Local brush placement.  
* Texture baking above Lite’s 1024×1024 cap.  
* More noise methods.  
* More texturing methods.  
* More texture inputs.  
* Runtime MonoBehaviour tools and a fuller runtime-oriented setup.  
* A material and texture atlas combiner for rocks and other assets.

Lite can still produce usable rocks, but it has deliberate limits. Those limits become more important when you need large rock libraries, higher-resolution texture output, stronger placement tools, broader material variation, or more organized optimization workflows.

For example, Lite’s sedimentary strata system uses procedural color bands. That is enough for readable sandstone, shale, canyon, and layered cliff-style rocks, but Pro is the better choice when you need richer texture-based layer workflows, more texture inputs, and more advanced material blending.

Lite is the free starting point. Pro is the recommended version for serious environment production.

---

### **Using Lite and Pro in the Same Project**

Rock Generator Lite and Rock Generator Pro use different namespaces and assemblies, so they can safely exist in the same Unity project.

That said, if you already own Rock Generator Pro, you should generally remove the Lite version unless you have a specific reason to keep both. Pro covers the Lite workflow and adds the fuller toolset. Keeping both installed is supported, but it can make menus, examples, generated folders, and project organization more cluttered than necessary.

The Lite package is organized into separate runtime, editor, demo runtime, and demo editor assemblies. Editor assemblies are placed in Editor folders and are excluded from player builds. Runtime assemblies remain separate from the Editor tools.

Do not treat this as “no runtime code exists.” Lite does include runtime-side generation infrastructure because the Editor tools use the same procedural mesh generation pipeline internally. The normal recommended workflow, however, is still Editor-first: generate rocks in the Editor, save them as prefabs, and use those prefabs in scenes and builds.

### **Basic Requirements**

Rock Generator Lite is designed for Unity 6 or newer.

Texture baking uses Compute Shaders, so your project and target platform need Compute Shader support if you want to use Procedural Texture Bake or Triplanar Input Bake modes. Mesh generation also relies on Unity’s Burst, Collections, Jobs, and Mathematics packages.

The main recommended workflow is:

1. Generate rocks in the Editor.  
2. Save them as prefabs.  
3. Use those prefabs in scenes and builds.

Runtime texture baking is not part of the Lite workflow. If runtime generation is an important part of your project, Rock Generator Pro is the better fit because it includes a fuller runtime-oriented setup.

## **2\. Quick Start: Generate Your First Rock**

This section covers the shortest practical path to generating a rock prefab with Rock Generator Lite. It is meant as a first pass through the tool, not a full explanation of every setting.

For your first rock, do not start by manually changing every noise and texture value. Use a preset, change the seed, make a few controlled edits, preview the result, then generate a prefab.

Rock Generator Lite is good for learning the workflow and creating individual rocks. Rock Generator Pro is the recommended version for ongoing production use, especially if you need batch generation, higher texture resolutions, richer texturing, more texture inputs, a stronger placement workflow, local brush placement, runtime MonoBehaviour tools, or material and texture atlas combining.

---

### **Open the Rock Window**

Open the Rock Window from the Unity menu:

**Tools \> Veridian \> Rock Generator Lite \> 1\. Rock Window**

The Rock Window can also be opened from:

**GameObject \> 3D Object \> Veridian Rock Lite**

The GameObject menu item opens the generator window. It does not immediately create a finished rock in the scene. Finished rocks are created when you generate and save a prefab.

When the Rock Window opens without a saved profile assigned, it creates a temporary in-memory profile. This lets you test settings without first creating a RockSettings asset in your project.

---

### **Set the Output Information**

In the left settings panel, open **General & Output**.

Set the main output fields:

**Output Directory**  
 The project folder where generated rock assets will be saved.

**Export Name**  
 The base name used for the generated rock.

**Base Mesh Shape**  
 The starting topology used by the generator.

**Rock Type**  
 A broad setup shortcut for the rock’s procedural behavior.

**Seed**  
 The value used to create a specific variation.

The output directory should be inside the Unity **Assets** folder. If the folder does not already exist, the generator will attempt to create it.

Each generated rock is saved into its own generated folder. Depending on the selected settings, that folder may contain the prefab, mesh assets, material, textures, and collider mesh data.

Use a dedicated output folder for generated rocks. This keeps test output separate from other project assets and makes cleanup easier.

---

### **Choose a Starting Setup**

There are several ways to begin:

* Use the default temporary profile.  
* Choose a Rock Type such as Igneous, Metamorphic, or Sedimentary.  
* Apply a Quick Start Preset.  
* Assign an existing RockSettings asset in the Base Asset field.

For a first rock, use a **Quick Start Preset**.

The Rock Type setting is a broad shortcut. It changes selected noise and pattern settings to create a general procedural behavior. For example, Sedimentary enables terracing and switches to a strata color pattern, while Igneous emphasizes Voronoi-style breakup.

Quick Start Presets are more specific. They represent named rock styles such as sandstone, obsidian, basalt, granite, shale, pumice, quartz, marble, mineral-bearing rocks, and stylized fantasy materials.

Applying a preset changes the active profile settings and randomizes the seed.

Presets should be treated as starting points. After applying one, you can still adjust shape, noise, color, texture, LOD, and collider settings manually.

---

### **Recommended First Preset Workflow**

For the first few tests, use this order:

1. Apply a preset that is close to the rock style you want.  
2. Press **New Seed** until the silhouette looks useful.  
3. Adjust **Prefab Scale** if you only want the rock larger or smaller in the scene.  
4. Adjust proportions if the rock needs to be flatter, taller, wider, or more slab-like.  
5. Preview the baked texture result.  
6. Generate the prefab.

Avoid making heavy changes to macro noise, detail noise, Voronoi, terracing, bump, micro-detail, smoothness, and metallic settings all at once. Those systems interact. If the rock starts looking worse, it becomes hard to tell which setting caused the problem.

A good procedural rock usually comes from a controlled sequence: silhouette first, medium shape second, color third, bump and material response last.

---

### **Understand the Two Size Controls**

Rock Generator Lite has two important size controls:

**Target Diameter (Meters)**  
 This changes the approximate generated mesh size before prefab scale is applied.

**Prefab Scale**  
 This applies a final uniform scale to the generated prefab/LOD children after the rock has already been generated.

These are not the same thing.

Most included presets are authored around a **2 meter target diameter**. In many cases, it is better to leave Target Diameter near 2 meters and change **Prefab Scale** when you only want a larger or smaller version of the same rock.

This matters because Target Diameter is used by diameter-relative systems such as baked bump and micro-detail scale limits. Changing Target Diameter can also change how some procedural details feel relative to the rock. Surface patterns may read broader, tighter, softer, or harsher than they did in the preset.

Use this rule of thumb:

**Use Prefab Scale** when you want simple scene-size variation.  
 For example, use the same 2 meter generated rock as a 0.5× scatter stone, a 1× boulder, or a 2× larger prop.

**Use Target Diameter** when you intentionally want to regenerate the rock as a different physical size and accept that procedural relationships may change.  
 This can be useful, but it is a design choice rather than a simple scale operation.

Changing the prefab scale after generation can also be useful when scattering rocks, because the same generated prefab can appear at different sizes without creating a new baked texture set for each instance.

---

### **Preview the Rock**

The right side of the Rock Window contains the preview viewport.

Preview controls:

**Left Mouse Drag or Right Mouse Drag**  
 Rotates the preview camera.

**Mouse Wheel**  
 Changes the zoom distance.

**Reset Camera**  
 Frames the current rock in the viewport.

**New Seed**  
 Creates a new variation using the current settings.

The preview updates when settings change. It is generated as a temporary preview object and is not saved into the project until a prefab is generated.

If the preview becomes hard to inspect after changing proportions, size, or displacement, use **Reset Camera**.

If the preview appears stale or visually corrupted, use **Refresh** to rebuild the temporary preview state.

---

### **Inspect LODs**

The top of the preview area includes a **View LOD** dropdown. Each entry shows the LOD index and an approximate triangle count.

Use this to check how the generated rock changes across its LOD levels. Lower LODs should usually preserve the broad silhouette while reducing mesh density.

Do not judge only LOD 0\. A rock may look good up close but collapse into an awkward shape at a lower LOD if the density settings are too aggressive.

The final prefab uses a Unity LODGroup based on the LOD settings in the active profile.

---

### **Choose the Output Texturing Method**

Open **Procedural Texturing & Bump** and choose an **Output Texturing Method**.

Rock Generator Lite supports three output modes.

---

#### **Vertex Colors**

Vertex Colors stores procedural color data in the mesh vertex colors.

This avoids generated texture files and can be useful for lightweight assets, mobile games, stylized projects, or runtime-oriented experiments where baked textures are not desired.

However, Vertex Color mode is lower fidelity than baked texture output. It depends heavily on mesh density and on the material or shader being able to display vertex colors correctly. If the shader does not read vertex colors, the rock may appear plain or incorrectly colored.

The included presets are primarily authored for baked texture output. There are no dedicated Vertex Color presets. You can apply a normal preset and switch it to Vertex Colors as an approximation, but expect the result to be simpler and less detailed than the baked version.

---

#### **Procedural Texture Bake**

Procedural Texture Bake generates texture maps from the procedural color, normal, cavity, metallic, smoothness, and related settings.

This is the standard baked texture workflow in Lite and is the recommended option for a first test.

It creates a regular material and texture set for the generated prefab. This usually gives better-looking results than Vertex Color mode, especially for rocks that depend on bump, micro-detail, strata, cavity shading, metallic deposits, or smoothness variation.

---

#### **Triplanar Input Bake**

Triplanar Input Bake uses assigned input albedo and normal textures, projects them onto the generated rock, combines them with procedural overlays, and bakes the result into texture maps.

This is useful when you already have tiling rock textures and want to bake them into a generated rock prefab.

Lite supports only a limited custom texture input workflow. Rock Generator Pro is the better choice when you need more texture inputs, richer texture blending, texture-splatted layered materials, or broader material authoring options.

---

### **Preview Baked Textures**

When using a baked texture mode, the preview can generate temporary textures for the viewport. These preview textures are not saved to disk.

The preview may refresh automatically after a short delay when settings change. This prevents the tool from rebaking temporary textures on every small slider movement.

Use **Force Update Colors** when you want to manually refresh the baked preview after changing color, bump, micro-detail, AO, metallic, smoothness, or related material settings.

The preview quality setting controls the temporary bake resolution used in the Rock Window.

Rock Generator Lite supports texture baking up to:

**1024×1024**

The interface may show 2048 and 4096 entries as Pro options, but those are not available in Lite.

While designing the shape, lower preview resolutions are usually faster to work with. Use 1024 when you need a closer look at the baked material result.

Rock Generator Pro supports higher-resolution baking and is the better choice when you need 2K or 4K rock textures.

---

### **Use Bump and Micro-Detail Carefully**

For baked rocks, procedural bump is usually worth using. It adds lighting detail without changing the mesh silhouette and helps many rocks feel less flat.

However, bump and micro-detail should usually be tuned after the main shape and color already look good.

Use geometry noise when the silhouette needs more form. Use bump when the surface needs more lighting detail. Use micro-detail when the rock needs fine grit, pores, chalkiness, pumice texture, or rough mineral grain.

If bump is too strong, the rock can look noisy, crunchy, glittery, or artificially sharpened. If smoothness is also too high, the result can look like plastic, wet glass, or polished resin instead of stone.

For most baked rocks:

* Keep baked normal strength near a normal/default value.  
* Use bump at moderate strength.  
* Add micro-detail only when the material needs fine grain.  
* Reduce smoothness for dry stone unless the preset is intentionally wet, glassy, polished, or metallic.

The older bump-scale issue warned about in some tooltip language has been largely addressed by diameter-relative scale handling, but bump is still a powerful visual control. It can improve most rocks, and it can also make a good rock look worse if pushed too far.

---

### **Review LOD and Collider Settings**

Before generating the prefab, review:

**LOD Settings**  
 Controls the generated mesh levels used by the final LODGroup.

**Physics & Collisions**  
 Controls whether the prefab receives a collider and what type of collider is used.

The default LOD setup is enough for a first test.

For colliders, **Convex Mesh** is usually the safest starting point. It gives a reasonably shaped collider while avoiding the cost and restrictions of exact non-convex collision.

Use **Primitive Box** or **Primitive Sphere** for background rocks, simple debris, or cases where collision accuracy is not important.

Use **Exact Mesh** only for static environment pieces where detailed collision matters, such as a large cliff section, walkable rock surface, or a boulder where the exact shape is important. Exact Mesh colliders are non-convex and should not be treated as the default for normal physics objects.

---

### **Generate the Prefab**

Use **Generate Prefab (Save to Project)** when the rock is ready.

The generator creates the meshes, material, textures if needed, LOD setup, and collider setup, then saves the result into the selected output folder.

Depending on the selected settings, the generated folder may include:

* The generated prefab.  
* LOD mesh assets.  
* A material.  
* Baked albedo and normal textures.  
* A packed mask map or individual auxiliary maps.  
* Height, AO, smoothness, or metallic maps when enabled.  
* Collider mesh data when needed.

After generation, the saved prefab can be placed in a scene, duplicated, used with the Rock Placer, or organized with the rest of your environment assets.

---

### **Remove an Accidental Generation**

After a prefab has been generated, the Rock Window may show **Undo Last Generation**.

This removes the folder created for the most recently generated rock. It is intended for cleaning up test assets that you do not want to keep.

This operation deletes saved project assets from disk. It is separate from Unity’s normal scene Undo workflow.

Use it carefully.

---

### **First Rock Checklist**

For a clean first test:

1. Open the Rock Window.  
2. Choose a Quick Start Preset.  
3. Press New Seed until the silhouette looks useful.  
4. Leave Target Diameter near 2 meters unless you specifically want to change generated scale relationships.  
5. Use Prefab Scale for simple size variation.  
6. Use Procedural Texture Bake.  
7. Preview at a lower resolution while editing.  
8. Use 1024 preview quality for a final check.  
9. Keep Convex Mesh as the collider type.  
10. Generate the prefab.

This gives you a normal Unity prefab that can be placed, duplicated, scattered, or reused like other environment props.

## **3\. Demo Orchestrator: Test the Lite Workflow**

The Demo Orchestrator is a guided test workflow for Rock Generator Lite. It can create a temporary Unity Terrain, generate a demo rock prefab, and send that prefab to the Rock Placer.

Use the Demo Orchestrator when you want to test the Lite asset without first building your own terrain, organizing output folders, or setting up a scatter scene by hand.

The Demo Orchestrator is not meant to replace a production terrain system. It is a quick way to see the basic Rock Generator Lite loop:

1. Generate a terrain canvas.  
2. Generate a rock.  
3. Send the rock to the placer.  
4. Scatter it on the terrain.

This is also a useful preview of the broader workflow Rock Generator Pro is designed to expand. If you find yourself wanting to generate many rocks, compare batches, place them with more control, paint them locally, or optimize a larger rock set, that is where the Pro version becomes the recommended workflow.

---

### **Open the Demo Orchestrator**

Open the Demo Orchestrator from the Unity menu:

**Tools \> Veridian \> Rock Generator Lite \> 2\. Demo Orchestrator**

The window is organized into three main stages:

1. Generate an environment.  
2. Generate a demo rock.  
3. Send the generated rock to the Rock Placer.

The Demo Orchestrator can also be opened from the included demo readme inspector shortcuts if you are using the sample/demo scene content.

---

### **What the Demo Orchestrator Is For**

The Demo Orchestrator is best used for:

* First-time testing.  
* Checking that the generator, baker, and placer are working correctly.  
* Quickly trying included rock presets.  
* Creating a simple scene where rocks can be scattered immediately.  
* Understanding the Lite workflow before deciding whether to move to Pro.

It is not intended for:

* Final terrain authoring.  
* Large production rock libraries.  
* Batch generation.  
* Advanced placement control.  
* Local brush placement.  
* Runtime generation workflows.  
* Atlas or material combining.

Rock Generator Pro is the better version for those larger workflows. Lite gives you the core generation loop; Pro is intended to make that loop more practical at production scale.

### **Global Output Settings**

At the top of the window, set the **Demo Output Path**.

The default path is:

**Assets/VeridianData/RockGenLite/Demo\_Assets**

The path should stay inside the Unity **Assets** folder. If you use **Browse**, choose a folder under Assets so Unity can import the generated content.

The Demo Orchestrator creates and uses these subfolders:

**Textures**  
 Stores the simple procedural textures generated for the demo terrain.

**Layers**  
 Stores the generated Terrain Layer assets.

**Rocks**  
 Stores demo rock prefabs and their generated mesh/material/texture output.

For most users, the default path is the safest choice. Keeping demo output in a clearly named demo folder prevents test files from mixing with production rocks.

It is also best to keep the word **Demo** in the output path. The purge operation uses that word as part of its safety check before it allows deletion.

---

### **Stage 1: Generate an Environment**

The first stage creates a temporary Unity Terrain for testing generated rocks.

Use **Generate Realistic Terrain Canvas** to create the terrain.

The generated terrain includes:

* A TerrainData asset.  
* A Terrain GameObject in the current scene.  
* Procedural Grass, Dirt, and Stone textures.  
* Terrain Layers using those textures.  
* Height variation from layered noise.  
* Slope-based terrain painting.

This is a test canvas. It gives you hills, slopes, cliffs, and flatter areas so you can immediately see how generated rocks behave when scattered. It is not a geological terrain simulator and should not be treated as a final environment tool.

---

### **Environment Settings**

The terrain controls are simple, but they are still useful for testing different placement situations.

**Terrain Size**  
 Controls the terrain width and length. Larger sizes give the placer more room but also create a larger test surface. The default size is usually enough for testing.

**Height Multiplier**  
 Controls the vertical scale of the terrain. Low values create softer terrain. Higher values create taller hills and steeper slopes. If you want to test slope restrictions in the Rock Placer, use enough height variation to create visible cliffs or ridges.

---

### **Cliff Fractal Settings**

The terrain noise controls affect the terrain canvas, not the generated rock itself.

**Noise Scale**  
 Controls the size of the terrain features. Lower values create broader hills and valleys. Higher values create more frequent terrain variation.

**Octaves**  
 Controls how many layers of terrain noise are combined. More octaves add secondary detail, but high values can make the test terrain busier.

**Persistence**  
 Controls how strongly later noise layers contribute. Higher values make smaller terrain detail more visible.

**Lacunarity**  
 Controls how quickly the noise frequency increases between octaves. Higher values can create sharper or more broken terrain variation.

**Ridge Strength**  
 Adds stronger ridge-like variation to the terrain. Use this when you want steeper rocky areas for testing slope limits and alignment.

For a first test, keep the defaults. After confirming the workflow, adjust Height Multiplier and Ridge Strength if you want a more dramatic scatter surface.

---

### **Replacing Older Demo Terrain**

When you generate a new demo terrain, the tool checks for older terrains whose TerrainData asset is stored under the current demo output path.

If it finds those older demo terrains, it removes their scene GameObjects before creating the new one. This helps prevent the scene from collecting old demo terrain objects.

This cleanup applies to demo terrains associated with the current demo path. It is not a general scene cleanup command and does not delete unrelated terrains.

---

### **Stage 2: Generate a Demo Rock**

The second stage creates one demo rock prefab.

You can generate the rock from either:

**A built-in demo preset**  
 Choose a preset from the preset buttons or the Demo Preset dropdown.

**A custom RockSettings profile**  
 Assign your own profile to **OR Custom Profile**.

If a custom profile is assigned, the tool uses a temporary clone of that profile. If no custom profile is assigned, the tool creates a temporary RockSettings object and applies the selected preset.

In both cases, the demo generation uses a random seed so the result is a variation rather than the exact same rock every time.

The generated rock is saved under:

**\[Demo Output Path\]/Rocks**

The demo rock uses the same prefab generation system as the Rock Window. Depending on the profile settings, it may generate meshes, a prefab, material, baked textures, LODs, and collider data.

---

### **Presets in the Demo Orchestrator**

The Demo Orchestrator shows the included Quick Start Presets as selectable buttons.

These presets are not just menu decorations. They are the fastest way to learn what the generator can do. For example, a sandstone preset shows how terracing and strata coloring can work together, while a volcanic or metallic preset may rely more heavily on Voronoi, darker cavity coloring, smoothness, or metallic overlays.

For the best first test:

1. Pick a preset with a clear visual identity.  
2. Bake the demo rock.  
3. Send it to the Rock Placer.  
4. Scatter it on the generated terrain.  
5. Return to the Rock Window later if you want to customize the profile in more detail.

Do not judge the generator only from one seed. Some procedural rocks need a few seed changes before the silhouette feels useful.

---

### **Custom Profile Behavior**

Assigning a custom RockSettings profile is useful when you have already designed a rock in the Rock Window and want to test it quickly on terrain.

The Demo Orchestrator clones the custom profile before generation. That means the demo generation can randomize the seed and change demo output settings without directly editing your original profile asset.

This is useful for safe testing, but it also means that changes made by the Demo Orchestrator are not a replacement for intentionally editing and saving the profile in the Rock Window.

---

### **Active Demo Rock Handoff**

When a new demo rock is baked, the Demo Orchestrator clears its current internal handoff list and adds the newly generated prefab.

This means **Push to Rock Placer Window** is intended to send the most recently baked demo rock to the Rock Placer.

Older generated demo prefabs may still remain in the demo output folder until you purge the demo assets, but they are not necessarily kept in the current handoff list.

Use this behavior to keep the demo flow simple: bake one rock, push it to the placer, test it, then bake another if needed.

---

### **Stage 3: Send the Demo Rock to the Rock Placer**

After a demo rock has been generated, use:

**Push to Rock Placer Window**

This opens the Rock Placer and adds the generated rock prefab to the placer’s prefab palette.

The Demo Orchestrator also tries to assign the current demo terrain as the Rock Placer’s target terrain. It looks for a terrain whose TerrainData asset is stored under the current demo output path. If it cannot find that terrain, it falls back to another Terrain in the open scene if one is available.

The Rock Placer can then scatter the generated prefab on the terrain using placement settings such as:

* Spawn count.  
* Prefab weights.  
* Scale range.  
* Slope limits.  
* Height limits.  
* Surface alignment.  
* Alignment blend.  
* Vertical sink offset.  
* Perlin-noise clumping.

The Rock Placer is covered in more detail later in this documentation.

---

### **Handoff Does Not Delete or Replace Your Rock Assets**

Sending a rock to the Rock Placer does not delete the generated prefab, bake a new prefab, or modify the generated rock asset on disk.

It simply opens the Rock Placer and adds the generated prefab to the placer palette.

The generated prefab remains saved in the demo output folder. You can still find it in the Project window, inspect it, duplicate it, or move it like any other generated Unity prefab.

---

### **Purging Demo Assets**

The Demo Orchestrator includes a purge operation for generated demo content.

Use:

**Purge All Demo Assets**

This is meant for cleaning up the test terrain, demo textures, terrain layers, and demo rocks created by the Demo Orchestrator.

Before deleting files, the tool checks that the Demo Output Path:

* Is not empty.  
* Is not the root **Assets** folder.  
* Contains the word **Demo**.

If the path fails those checks, the purge is aborted.

If the path passes those checks and you accept the confirmation dialog, the tool deletes the demo output folder and removes demo terrain GameObjects whose TerrainData assets are stored under that path.

It also clears the current demo prefab handoff list in the Demo Orchestrator.

This operation deletes project assets from disk. Use it only for generated demo content.

---

### **Recommended Demo Flow**

For a clean first test:

1. Keep the default Demo Output Path.  
2. Generate the terrain canvas.  
3. Choose a clear demo preset, such as a sandstone, basalt, granite, pumice, or mineral preset.  
4. Bake the demo rock.  
5. Push the generated rock to the Rock Placer.  
6. Use the Rock Placer to populate the demo terrain.  
7. Adjust scale, slope limits, height limits, alignment, and clumping.  
8. Purge the demo assets when the test content is no longer needed.

This keeps temporary files separate from production output and lets you test the full Lite pipeline quickly.

---

### **When the Demo Workflow Starts Feeling Too Small**

The Demo Orchestrator is intentionally limited. It is there to help you try the generator, not to manage a full environment production pass.

Move to Rock Generator Pro when you want to do this workflow repeatedly or at larger scale.

Rock Generator Pro is the recommended version if you need:

* Batch rock generation.  
* More production-focused placement tools.  
* Local brush placement.  
* Higher texture bake resolutions.  
* More noise and texturing methods.  
* More custom texture inputs.  
* Runtime MonoBehaviour tools.  
* A material and texture atlas combiner for rocks and other assets.

A practical way to think about it:

**Lite lets you test and create individual rocks.**  
 **Pro is the version meant to build, place, and manage rock sets more seriously.**

## **4\. The Rock Window**

The Rock Window is the main workspace for creating individual rocks in Rock Generator Lite. It is where you edit rock settings, preview generated geometry, inspect LODs, preview baked textures, save profiles, and generate prefabs.

Open it from the Unity menu:

**Tools \> Veridian \> Rock Generator Lite \> 1\. Rock Window**

You can also open it from:

**GameObject \> 3D Object \> Veridian Rock Lite**

The GameObject menu item opens the same Rock Window. It does not directly place a finished rock into the scene. Rocks are generated as saved prefabs after you configure them and use **Generate Prefab (Save to Project)**.

---

### **What the Rock Window Is For**

Use the Rock Window when you want to:

* Create a new rock from a temporary profile.  
* Load and edit a saved RockSettings profile.  
* Apply a Quick Start Preset.  
* Change the seed to find a better variation.  
* Tune shape, proportions, noise, color, texturing, LOD, and collider settings.  
* Preview the rock before saving.  
* Bake texture previews.  
* Generate a clean Unity prefab.

The Rock Window is the most important tool in Lite. The Demo Orchestrator is for quick testing; the Rock Window is where you actually design the rock.

---

### **Window Layout**

The Rock Window is split into two main areas.

The left side contains the settings panel. This is where you edit the active rock profile, choose presets, set output paths, adjust shape and texture options, configure LODs, choose collider settings, save profiles, and generate the final prefab.

The right side contains the preview viewport. This shows the generated rock and lets you inspect its shape, material preview, LODs, and wireframe overlay.

The divider between the settings panel and preview panel can be dragged horizontally. Use this when you need more room for the settings list or a larger preview.

A practical workflow is to keep the preview large while judging silhouette and LODs, then expand the settings side when tuning detailed values.

---

### **Temporary and Saved Profiles**

Rock Generator Lite uses **RockSettings** ScriptableObjects to store rock profiles.

A profile contains the settings used to generate a rock, including:

* Output folder and export name.  
* Base mesh shape.  
* Rock type.  
* Seed.  
* Target diameter and prefab scale.  
* Proportions.  
* Macro and detail noise.  
* Domain warping, Voronoi, and terracing.  
* Procedural texturing.  
* Bump and micro-detail.  
* Texture export settings.  
* LOD settings.  
* Collider settings.

When the Rock Window opens without a profile assigned, it creates a temporary in-memory profile. This lets you experiment without first creating extra project assets.

Temporary profiles are useful for testing, but they should not be treated as saved work. If you create something you want to keep, use **Save As New Asset...**.

---

### **Editing a Saved Profile**

When you assign an existing RockSettings asset to the **Base Asset** field, the Rock Window edits a temporary copy of that asset.

This is important.

Changes made in the Rock Window do not automatically overwrite the original profile asset. This lets you test a preset, change the seed, or push settings around without immediately damaging a saved profile.

Use:

**Apply to '\[Profile Name\]'**  
 Writes the current preview settings back to the assigned RockSettings asset.

**Save As...**  
 Creates a new RockSettings asset from the current edited settings.

You can also drag a RockSettings asset into the Rock Window to load it as the active base asset.

For safer editing, use **Save As...** before making large changes. This is especially useful before applying presets, because applying a preset overwrites many settings at once.

---

### **Base Asset Field**

The **Base Asset** field controls whether you are editing a saved profile or a temporary profile.

If the field is empty, the window is editing transient settings. These settings exist only as the current working state until you save them as an asset.

If the field contains a RockSettings asset, the window is editing a temporary copy of that asset. The original asset is not changed until you use **Apply to '\[Profile Name\]'**.

Use this distinction deliberately:

* Use transient settings for quick experiments.  
* Use saved profiles when you want reusable procedural recipes.  
* Use Save As when you want to branch a profile into a new variation.  
* Use Apply only when you are sure the source profile should be updated.

---

### **Quick Start Guide Foldout**

At the top of the settings panel, the Rock Window includes a **Quick Start Guide** foldout.

This is a small in-window reminder of the basic workflow:

1. Design the rock using the generation and texturing parameters on the left.  
2. Rotate and zoom the preview on the right.  
3. Use baked texture preview modes when working with Procedural Texture Bake or Triplanar Input Bake.  
4. Use **Force Update Colors** when the baked texture preview does not appear to update after changing material settings.  
5. Use **Refresh** if the preview appears visually corrupted.  
6. Use **Generate Prefab** when the rock is ready to export.

The foldout is only a reminder. The full workflow is described in the Quick Start section.

---

### **Toolbar Controls**

The toolbar at the top of the settings panel contains three common controls.

**Refresh**  
 Rebuilds the temporary preview state. Use this if the preview appears visually corrupted, stale, or out of sync. Refresh is stronger than just changing the seed; it rebuilds the temporary preview renderer, generator state, material state, mesh instance, wireframe material, and baked preview texture references.

**New Seed**  
 Assigns a new random seed to the active profile and regenerates the preview. This is the fastest way to get a different rock variation while keeping the same overall style.

For most presets, changing the seed should be your first customization step. It can change the silhouette, proportions if randomized proportions are enabled, noise placement, color variation, and other procedural details.

**Reset Camera**  
 Frames the current generated rock in the preview viewport. Use this after changing Target Diameter, Prefab Scale, proportions, or displacement settings.

---

### **Quick Start Presets**

The settings panel includes a **Quick Start Presets** area. This lets you choose a preset from the dropdown and apply it to the active profile.

Applying a preset changes the current profile settings and randomizes the seed.

Presets are separate from the **Rock Type** setting.

**Rock Type** is a broad procedural shortcut, such as Igneous, Metamorphic, or Sedimentary.

**Quick Start Presets** are more specific authored starting points, such as sandstone, obsidian, basalt, granite, shale, pumice, quartz, marble, or mineral-bearing styles.

Use presets as editable examples. They are useful because procedural rock settings interact in non-obvious ways. A preset shows a working combination of shape, color, bump, smoothness, metallic, strata, or noise settings.

A good workflow is:

1. Apply a preset.  
2. Change the seed.  
3. Adjust proportions or prefab scale.  
4. Tune one setting group at a time.  
5. Save the result as a new profile if it becomes useful.

Do not apply presets casually over a profile you care about unless you have saved a copy. A preset overwrites many values in the current working settings.

---

### **Rock Type vs Quick Start Preset**

The Rock Type field and Quick Start Presets solve different problems.

**Rock Type** changes broad procedural behavior:

* Igneous emphasizes Voronoi-style breakup and stronger eroded detail behavior.  
* Metamorphic emphasizes domain warping and ridged detail.  
* Sedimentary emphasizes terracing and strata coloring.  
* Custom leaves the settings under manual control.

These categories are useful when you want a broad geological direction, but they are not full simulations. They are setup shortcuts.

**Quick Start Presets** are more complete rock recipes. They configure shape, proportions, color pattern, bump, material response, metallic settings, and other controls depending on the preset.

For most users, Quick Start Presets are the better place to begin.

---

### **General & Output**

The **General & Output** foldout controls where the generated prefab will be saved and what broad setup it uses.

Important fields include:

**Output Directory**  
 The project folder where generated rocks will be saved.

**Export Name**  
 The base name used for the generated asset folder and files.

**Base Mesh Shape**  
 The starting topology used by the generator.

**Rock Type**  
 A broad procedural setup shortcut.

**Seed**  
 The value used to generate a specific variation.

Use an output directory inside the Unity **Assets** folder. If the folder does not exist, the generator attempts to create it.

Generated rocks are saved into unique folders so existing output is not accidentally overwritten.

---

### **Core Shape & Sizing**

The **Core Shape & Sizing** foldout controls the basic size and proportions of the generated rock.

Two settings are especially important:

**Target Diameter (Meters)**  
 Changes the generated mesh size before prefab scale is applied.

**Prefab Scale**  
 Applies a final scale to the finished prefab/LOD children after the mesh has already been generated.

For most preset-based workflows, keep Target Diameter near the authored preset scale, usually around **2 meters**, and use Prefab Scale for simple scene-size variation.

Changing Target Diameter can affect diameter-relative systems such as baked bump and micro-detail scale limits. It can also change how some procedural details feel relative to the rock.

Changing Prefab Scale is safer when you simply want the same rock to appear larger or smaller in the scene.

---

### **Noise Configuration**

The **Noise Configuration** foldout contains the main shape controls: macro noise, detail noise, domain warping, Voronoi, and terracing.

This foldout is collapsed by default because beginners should usually start from presets rather than adjusting every noise control manually.

Open it when you want direct control over the shape.

A practical editing order is:

1. Adjust proportions first.  
2. Adjust macro shape second.  
3. Adjust detail displacement third.  
4. Add domain warping, Voronoi, or terracing only when needed.  
5. Avoid making several strong noise changes at the same time.

If the rock becomes too spiky, melted, blobby, or chaotic, reduce displacement strength and modifier intensity before changing every setting.

The shape and noise controls are explained in more detail in Section 5\.

---

### **Procedural Texturing & Bump**

The **Procedural Texturing & Bump** foldout controls how the generated rock receives color, material data, baked normal detail, auxiliary maps, and metallic or mineral effects.

This is one of the most important sections in the Rock Window, and also one of the least intuitive if used randomly.

The texturing system is layered. Color pattern, cavity color, slope placement, strata warp, patch noise, bump, micro-detail, AO, smoothness, metallic overlays, and triplanar input textures can all affect the final result.

For a good first workflow:

1. Choose a preset.  
2. Make sure the shape reads well.  
3. Adjust main colors.  
4. Adjust cavity color and cavity strength.  
5. Tune bump and micro-detail only after the color result is working.  
6. Keep smoothness low for dry stone unless the rock is intentionally wet, glassy, polished, or metallic.

Bump and micro-detail can improve many rocks, but high values can make the surface look noisy or artificial. This is especially visible under strong lighting.

The texturing and baking controls are explained in more detail in Section 6\.

---

### **Preview Viewport Controls**

The preview viewport shows the generated rock using the current settings.

Mouse controls:

**Left Mouse Drag or Right Mouse Drag**  
 Rotates the preview camera.

**Mouse Wheel**  
 Zooms in and out.

The preview camera orbits around the generated rock. If the rock becomes hard to see after changing scale, proportions, or displacement, use **Reset Camera**.

The preview is not a scene object. It is a temporary generated preview shown inside the Rock Window.

---

### **Preview Generation**

The preview is generated using the same runtime-side mesh generation infrastructure used by the rest of the Lite toolset.

When you change a setting, the preview is marked for regeneration. The Rock Window then rebuilds the temporary preview mesh and material state.

The preview object is hidden and unsaved. It is not added to your scene as a placed rock, and it is not saved into the project until you use **Generate Prefab (Save to Project)**.

This matters because it keeps experimentation safe. You can try different seeds, presets, and material settings without filling the Project window with unwanted meshes and textures.

---

### **Preview Textures vs Saved Textures**

When using **Procedural Texture Bake** or **Triplanar Input Bake**, the Rock Window can bake temporary preview textures.

These temporary preview textures exist only for the Rock Window. They are not saved to disk.

Final textures are created when you generate the prefab.

This means the preview is useful for judging the material, but it is not the final exported texture set. The final bake still happens during prefab generation.

---

### **Texture Preview Modes**

The preview behaves differently depending on the selected Output Texturing Method.

---

#### **Vertex Colors**

In Vertex Color mode, the preview displays generated vertex color data.

This mode avoids texture baking and can be useful for lightweight or mobile-style assets, but it is visually simpler than baked texture output. It also requires a material or shader that displays vertex colors correctly.

The included presets are primarily authored for baked texture output. You can switch a preset to Vertex Color mode as an approximation, but the result will usually be less detailed.

---

#### **Procedural Texture Bake**

In Procedural Texture Bake mode, the preview can bake temporary albedo, normal, mask, AO, metallic, and smoothness-style data for display in the viewport.

This is the standard visual workflow for Lite and usually gives the best-looking results.

Use this mode when judging cavity color, strata, organic patches, bump, micro-detail, smoothness, or metallic deposits.

---

#### **Triplanar Input Bake**

In Triplanar Input Bake mode, assigned input albedo and normal textures are projected across the generated rock and combined with procedural overlays.

This is useful when you have a tiling rock texture and want to bake it into the generated prefab.

Lite supports a smaller custom texture input workflow. Rock Generator Pro is the better choice when you need more texture inputs, richer blending, layered texture workflows, or more material authoring control.

---

### **Preview Quality**

The texture preview quality dropdown controls the temporary bake resolution used in the Rock Window.

Lite supports preview baking up to:

**1024×1024**

The dropdown may show 2048 and 4096 entries as Pro options. In Lite, selecting those resolutions shows a Pro feature notice and returns the preview bake resolution to 1024\.

Use lower preview resolutions while working on shape and broad color. Use 1024 when you need a closer look at the baked material result.

Rock Generator Pro supports higher-resolution baking and is the better choice when you need 2K or 4K rock textures.

---

### **Force Update Colors**

Use **Force Update Colors** to manually rebake the temporary preview textures.

This is useful after changing:

* Color pattern settings.  
* Cavity color.  
* Cavity strength.  
* Bump settings.  
* Micro-detail settings.  
* AO settings.  
* Metallic settings.  
* Smoothness settings.  
* Triplanar input texture settings.

The preview usually refreshes automatically after a short delay, but Force Update Colors gives you direct control when you want to confirm the current material result.

If the preview looks corrupted rather than merely out of date, use **Refresh** instead.

---

### **LOD Preview**

The top of the preview panel includes a **View LOD** dropdown.

Each entry shows the LOD index and an approximate triangle count, such as:

**LOD 0 (15360 Tris)**

Changing the selected LOD lets you inspect how each generated mesh tier looks.

This is useful because rock LODs are generated from different procedural primitive resolutions, not from a general mesh-collapse decimator. Lower LODs may lose small displacement features or simplify the silhouette more than expected.

A good rock should still read as the same general shape when viewed at lower LODs.

Check lower LODs before saving, especially for:

* Thin slabs.  
* Tall pillars.  
* Sharp basalt-like fragments.  
* Terraced sedimentary rocks.  
* Rocks with strong Voronoi breakup.  
* Rocks intended to be seen from multiple distances.

The final generated prefab uses a Unity LODGroup with the LOD settings from the active profile.

---

### **Wireframe Preview**

If your system supports geometry shaders, the preview toolbar includes a **Wireframe** option.

Available modes:

**Disabled**  
 No wireframe overlay.

**Black**  
 Displays a black wireframe overlay.

**White**  
 Displays a white wireframe overlay.

The wireframe overlay is for visual inspection only. It helps you check topology density, silhouette behavior, and how the LODs change.

It does not affect the generated prefab.

If your hardware or graphics API does not support geometry shaders, the wireframe option is disabled.

### **Asset Management**

The Asset Management area controls how the active profile and generated prefab are saved.

---

#### **Apply to '\[Profile Name\]'**

This appears when a saved RockSettings profile is assigned in the Base Asset field.

Use it to write the current temporary preview settings back into the original profile asset.

Use this deliberately. Once applied, the source profile asset is changed.

---

#### **Save As...**

This appears when a saved Base Asset is assigned.

Use it to create a new RockSettings asset from the current edited settings.

This is useful when you want to branch a profile. For example, you might start from Desert Sandstone, create a darker canyon variant, then save that as a separate profile.

---

#### **Save As New Asset...**

This appears when no saved Base Asset is assigned.

Use it to save the current transient settings as a new RockSettings asset.

Saved profiles are useful when you want to:

* Reuse a rock setup.  
* Generate multiple variations from the same base settings.  
* Keep a library of procedural profiles.  
* Return to a style later without rebuilding it from memory.

---

### **Generate Prefab**

Use **Generate Prefab (Save to Project)** when you are ready to create the final rock asset.

The generator creates an organized output folder and saves the generated assets there.

Depending on the selected settings, output can include:

* The generated prefab.  
* One mesh asset per visual LOD.  
* A collider mesh asset when needed.  
* A material.  
* Baked albedo texture.  
* Baked normal texture.  
* Packed mask map or individual auxiliary maps.  
* Height, AO, smoothness, or metallic maps when enabled.

The generated prefab contains child objects for the visual LODs and a Unity LODGroup on the root object. Depending on the collider settings, it may also contain a primitive collider, a convex mesh collider, or an exact mesh collider.

### **Undo Last Generation**

After a prefab is generated, the Rock Window may show an **Undo Last Generation** button.

Use this for accidental test output that you do not want to keep.

Use it carefully, especially if you generated into a folder that contains assets you still need.

### **Recommended Rock Window Workflow**

For controlled results, use this workflow:

1. Start with a Quick Start Preset.  
2. Press **New Seed** until the silhouette looks useful.  
3. Keep **Target Diameter** near the preset’s intended scale unless you intentionally want to change generated scale relationships.  
4. Use **Prefab Scale** for simple scene-size variation.  
5. Adjust proportions before noise.  
6. Adjust large shape before detail shape.  
7. Adjust color before bump.  
8. Use bump and micro-detail carefully.  
9. Check LODs.  
10. Choose a sensible collider.  
11. Generate the prefab.  
12. Save the profile if the setup is worth reusing.

This avoids the most common procedural workflow problem: changing too many interacting settings at once and losing track of what made the rock better or worse.

---

### **Common Rock Window Mistakes**

**Applying presets over an unsaved profile**  
 Applying a preset overwrites many settings. Save first if you want to keep the current setup.

**Changing Target Diameter when Prefab Scale would be better**  
 Use Prefab Scale when you only want the rock larger or smaller in the scene. Changing Target Diameter can affect procedural pattern relationships.

**Judging only LOD 0**  
 Always inspect lower LODs before generating important rocks.

**Overusing bump or micro-detail**  
 Bump can help most baked rocks, but too much makes the surface look noisy, glittery, or artificial.

**Using Vertex Color mode and expecting baked texture quality**  
 Vertex Color mode is useful for lightweight output, but it is usually lower fidelity than baked texture output and requires a shader that reads vertex colors.

**Using Exact Mesh colliders by default**  
 Exact Mesh colliders are non-convex and should be reserved for static detailed collision. Convex Mesh is usually the safer general-purpose choice.

**Generating many one-off baked materials in Lite**  
 Lite can create usable individual rocks, but large libraries of unique materials and textures are better handled with the Pro workflow, especially when atlas or material combining becomes important.

---

### **Summary**

The Rock Window is where most real work in Rock Generator Lite happens.

Use it to design a rock, preview it, save a reusable profile, and generate a clean prefab. Start from presets, change one group of settings at a time, and use the preview tools to check shape, material, LODs, and output before saving.

Rock Generator Lite gives you the core individual-rock workflow. Rock Generator Pro is the recommended upgrade when you need to turn that workflow into a larger production system.

## **5\. Designing Rock Shape and Noise**

Rock Generator Lite creates rock meshes from procedural settings. It starts with a base primitive, stretches or randomizes the proportions, applies procedural displacement, and then saves the result as one or more LOD meshes.

The basic shape pipeline is:

1. Choose a base mesh shape.  
2. Apply proportions.  
3. Apply Target Diameter.  
4. Add macro noise for large forms.  
5. Add detail noise for medium surface breakup.  
6. Optionally modify the detail noise with domain warping, Voronoi, or terracing.  
7. Apply Prefab Scale to the finished prefab/LOD children.

The generator is not a geological simulator. It does not simulate lava cooling, sediment deposition, metamorphic pressure, erosion, mineral growth, or real fracture mechanics. Instead, it uses noise and material rules to make rocks that are believable enough for game environments.

A useful way to think about it is:

**The shape controls make the rock read correctly from a distance.**  
 **The texture and bump controls make the surface read correctly under lighting.**

Do not try to fix a bad silhouette with bump maps. Bump can add surface detail, but it will not change the outline of the mesh. If the rock looks like a potato, sphere, melted blob, or broken procedural test object, fix the shape first.

Rock Generator Pro includes more noise methods and a broader procedural toolset. Lite gives you the core controls needed to make individual rocks, but Pro is the better choice when you need a larger production workflow with more generation variety.

---

### **Base Mesh Shape**

The **Base Mesh Shape** setting controls the starting topology before noise is applied.

Rock Generator Lite includes two base shapes:

---

#### **CubeSphere**

CubeSphere starts from a cube-like sphere built from six subdivided faces.

Use CubeSphere for:

* Blocky rocks.  
* Slabs.  
* Pillars.  
* Cliff chunks.  
* Sedimentary pieces.  
* Basalt-like fragments.  
* Shapes where flatter planes are useful.

CubeSphere is often a good choice when the rock should feel cut, stacked, fractured, columnar, or cliff-like. It tends to support flatter faces better than Icosphere, especially when used with terracing or blocky proportions.

CubeSphere density is controlled by **Grid Resolution** in the LOD settings.

---

#### **Icosphere**

Icosphere starts from a triangulated sphere.

Use Icosphere for:

* Rounded boulders.  
* River stones.  
* Mineral nodules.  
* Pumice-like chunks.  
* Granite boulders.  
* Quartz masses.  
* More organic rock forms.

Icosphere is usually better when the rock should feel naturally rounded, tumbled, knobbly, or uneven in all directions.

Icosphere density is controlled by **Subdivision Level** in the LOD settings.

---

### **Choosing Between CubeSphere and Icosphere**

Use **CubeSphere** when you want structure.

Use **Icosphere** when you want roundness.

For example:

* A sandstone slab usually works better as CubeSphere.  
* A mossy river boulder usually works better as Icosphere.  
* A basalt column fragment usually works better as CubeSphere.  
* A quartz nodule usually works better as Icosphere.  
* A cliff chunk can use either, depending on whether you want blocky faces or a more eroded silhouette.

The base shape does not force the final rock to look like a cube or sphere. Noise, proportions, terracing, and displacement still control the final result. But the starting topology affects how evenly displacement is distributed and how the lower LODs behave.

---

### **Size and Proportions**

The main size controls are in **Core Shape & Sizing**.

These controls matter more than they may first appear because they affect both the mesh shape and the way some procedural patterns feel.

---

#### **Target Diameter (Meters)**

**Target Diameter** changes the approximate generated mesh size before Prefab Scale is applied.

Most included presets are authored around a **2 meter target diameter**. That does not mean every rock must appear as a 2 meter rock in your scene. It means the preset’s procedural relationships were tuned with that generated size in mind.

For most preset-based work, start by leaving Target Diameter near 2 meters.

Changing Target Diameter can affect the way generated details feel because some systems are diameter-relative, especially baked bump and micro-detail scale limits. It can also make existing procedural patterns feel broader or tighter relative to the rock.

Use Target Diameter when you intentionally want to regenerate the rock as a different procedural size.

---

#### **Prefab Scale**

**Prefab Scale** applies a final scale to the generated prefab/LOD children after the mesh has been generated.

Use Prefab Scale when you simply want the finished rock to appear larger or smaller in a scene.

This is often the better option for ordinary size variation. For example, you can keep a preset authored around a 2 meter generated rock, then use Prefab Scale to make it appear as a smaller scatter stone, a normal boulder, or a larger prop.

Prefab Scale does not recalculate the procedural noise or baked bump relationships. It scales the finished result.

---

### **Recommended Scale Workflow**

For most users:

1. Leave **Target Diameter** near 2 meters.  
2. Use **Prefab Scale** for simple size variation.  
3. Change **Target Diameter** only when you intentionally want the procedural relationship between mesh size, bump, and detail to change.

This is especially important for baked rocks. A rock that looks good at a 2 meter generated size may not look the same if Target Diameter is pushed very small or very large, because the perceived scale of bump and surface detail changes.

There are cases where changing Target Diameter is useful. For example, a cliff chunk may benefit from being generated as a truly larger form rather than simply scaling a small boulder up. But that should be treated as a design choice, not as the default way to resize every rock.

---

### **Base Proportions**

**Base Proportions** stretch or squash the base shape before procedural displacement is applied.

Use proportions to establish the broad silhouette:

* Low Y values create flatter rocks or slabs.  
* High Y values create taller pillars or spires.  
* High X or Z values create elongated boulders.  
* Uneven X/Z values create more directional shapes.

This is usually the safest first shape edit after choosing a preset.

If the rock is supposed to be a shale slab, canyon shelf, or broken paving stone, make it flat with proportions before adding terracing or detail noise. If the rock is supposed to be an alpine spire, basalt fragment, or vent chimney, make it tall before adding aggressive displacement.

---

### **Randomize per Seed**

**Randomize per Seed** changes the base proportions using the current seed and the configured min/max limits.

This is useful when you want one profile to create multiple shape variations. For example, a boulder preset can generate wider, taller, flatter, or more compact versions without manually changing proportions each time.

Use this for:

* Scatter rocks.  
* Boulder libraries.  
* Minor shape variation.  
* Presets that should produce many usable silhouettes.

Avoid extreme min/max ranges unless you want unpredictable results. If the range allows very flat and very tall rocks at the same time, seed changes may produce results that no longer fit the original style.

The generator safely sorts reversed min/max values internally, so a reversed range will still produce a usable range. That does not mean every range will produce good art direction.

---

### **Rock Type**

The **Rock Type** setting provides a broad procedural setup for the current profile.

Available types:

* Igneous  
* Metamorphic  
* Sedimentary  
* Custom

These are not complete geological simulation modes. They are setup shortcuts that change selected noise and texturing parameters.

---

#### **Igneous**

Igneous enables Voronoi, disables domain warping and terracing, uses Swiss Erosion detail behavior, and sets Voronoi output to **F2 Minus F1**.

Use this when you want a rock that feels fractured, cooled, faceted, volcanic, or mineral-like.

It can work well for:

* Basalt-like chunks.  
* Obsidian-like fragments.  
* Dark volcanic rock.  
* Cracked igneous boulders.  
* Stylized crystalline breakup.

If the result becomes too cellular or artificial, reduce Voronoi Blend Intensity first.

---

#### **Metamorphic**

Metamorphic enables domain warping, disables Voronoi and terracing, and uses Ridged detail.

Use this when you want a folded, compressed, twisted, or banded look.

It can work well for:

* Schist-like forms.  
* Gneiss-like banded shapes.  
* Folded stone.  
* Stretched slabs.  
* Rocks that should feel squeezed or warped.

If the result starts to look melted, twisted like cloth, or too fluid, reduce Warp Strength.

---

#### **Sedimentary**

Sedimentary enables terracing, disables domain warping and Voronoi, switches the color pattern to **Sedimentary Strata**, and uses Ridged detail.

Use this when you want visible layers or ledges.

It can work well for:

* Sandstone.  
* Shale.  
* Canyon rocks.  
* Layered cliff blocks.  
* Limestone-like ledges.  
* Stratified slabs.

If the result looks like a staircase or low-poly construction prop, reduce Terrace Intensity or increase the number of terraces.

---

#### **Custom**

Custom is used when you want manual control.

If you edit the noise configuration after choosing a Rock Type, the profile may effectively become a custom setup. This is normal. Rock Type is there to get you started, not to lock the profile.

---

### **Quick Start Presets vs Rock Type**

Quick Start Presets are separate from Rock Type.

**Rock Type** is a broad category shortcut.

**Quick Start Presets** are more complete authored profiles. They usually configure shape, proportions, colors, texture pattern, bump, smoothness, metallic settings, and LOD behavior together.

For most users, Quick Start Presets are the better place to begin. They show combinations that already work. Then you can inspect and adjust the settings.

A good workflow is:

1. Apply a preset.  
2. Change the seed.  
3. Adjust proportions.  
4. Adjust Prefab Scale.  
5. Tune shape noise only if the silhouette needs it.  
6. Tune texturing and bump after the shape works.

Rock Generator Pro is the better choice when you need a larger set of production-oriented profiles, broader generation options, or batch generation of many rock variants.

---

### **Noise Configuration**

The **Noise Configuration** foldout contains the main procedural shape controls.

This foldout is collapsed by default because it is easy to make a rock worse by adjusting too many noise controls at once. Start from presets first. Open Noise Configuration when you know what part of the shape you are trying to change.

The Lite shape system has two main noise layers:

* Macro Noise  
* Detail Noise

Then it can apply optional modifiers:

* Domain Warping  
* Voronoi  
* Terracing

These systems interact. A setting that looks good on one preset may look bad on another because the base shape, proportions, scale, detail strength, color pattern, and LOD density are different.

---

### **Understanding Scale Controls**

Several noise controls are shown as **Scale** in the UI.

In practical terms:

**Larger scale values create broader, larger features.**  
 **Smaller scale values create tighter, smaller features.**

For example, a large Macro Scale creates broad bulges and uneven rock mass. A small Macro Scale creates tighter, more frequent lumps.

This is different from raw noise frequency in code. The UI is intended to be edited as a physical or visual scale, not as a mathematical frequency value.

When in doubt:

* Increase scale for broader features.  
* Decrease scale for smaller, busier features.  
* Reduce strength if the shape is becoming too extreme.

---

### **Macro Noise**

Macro Noise controls the large forms of the rock.

This is the noise layer that changes the broad silhouette. It can make the base shape feel eroded, broken, lumpy, asymmetric, or chunked.

Use Macro Noise when the rock feels too simple from a distance.

Macro Noise settings include:

**Fractal Type**  
 Controls how the macro noise is interpreted.

**Macro Scale**  
 Controls the size of the large forms. Larger scale values create broader forms. Smaller scale values create tighter, more fragmented forms.

**Displacement Strength**  
 Controls how strongly the macro noise pushes or pulls the mesh.

**Octaves**  
 Controls how many layers of macro noise are combined.

---

### **Practical Use of Macro Noise**

Macro Noise is usually the first noise layer to adjust.

Use it to make:

* A sphere less spherical.  
* A slab less perfectly flat.  
* A pillar less straight.  
* A boulder more eroded.  
* A cliff chunk less uniform.  
* A rock library less repetitive.

If Macro Noise is too weak, the rock may look like a stretched primitive.

If Macro Noise is too strong, the rock may look inflated, torn apart, melted, or impossible to use as a believable prop.

If Macro Scale is too small, the silhouette may become noisy and ragged instead of having clear large forms.

If Macro Scale is too large, the rock may have one or two big distortions but little interesting shape.

---

### **Detail Noise**

Detail Noise adds medium-scale surface variation on top of the base and macro shape.

This is geometry detail, not just normal-map detail. It can affect the mesh silhouette if the LOD density is high enough and the displacement is strong enough.

Detail Noise settings include:

**Fractal Type**  
 Controls how the detail noise is interpreted.

**Detail Scale**  
 Controls the size of the medium surface forms. Larger scale values create broader detail. Smaller scale values create tighter surface breakup.

**Displacement Strength**  
 Controls how strongly detail noise pushes and pulls the mesh.

**Octaves**  
 Controls how many layers of detail noise are combined.

**Lacunarity**  
 Controls how quickly detail frequency changes between octaves.

**Persistence**  
 Controls how strongly smaller octaves contribute.

---

### **Practical Use of Detail Noise**

Use Detail Noise after the broad shape is already working.

It is useful for:

* Broken stone surfaces.  
* Rough sandstone.  
* Pitted volcanic rock.  
* Eroded cliff chunks.  
* Jagged shale edges.  
* Granite-like irregularity.  
* General surface breakup.

If Detail Noise is too weak, the rock may look smooth, plastic, or primitive.

If Detail Noise is too strong, the rock can become spiky, noisy, or uncomfortable to use as a placed object.

If Detail Scale is too small and strength is high, the mesh may look like static, shredded foam, or procedural noise pasted onto a shape.

If Detail Scale is too large, it may compete with Macro Noise and make the rock feel lumpy rather than detailed.

---

### **Geometry Detail vs Bump Detail**

Detail Noise changes the mesh.

Bump and micro-detail change the baked normal map.

Use Detail Noise when the silhouette, edges, or actual geometry need more breakup.

Use Bump when the mesh silhouette is already good, but the surface looks too smooth under lighting.

This distinction matters. A rock with no geometric breakup but very strong bump often looks like a smooth object covered in noisy lighting. A rock with good geometry and moderate bump usually reads better.

---

### **Fractal Types**

Macro Noise and Detail Noise can use different fractal types.

The Lite shape system includes:

* Standard  
* Billow  
* Ridged  
* PingPong  
* Swiss Erosion

These are mathematical noise styles, but they can be understood visually.

---

#### **Standard**

Standard is the general-purpose noise mode.

It creates balanced positive and negative variation. Use it when you want a natural rough shape without a very specific ridge, fold, or bubble pattern.

Good for:

* General boulders.  
* Granite-like rocks.  
* Default scatter stones.  
* Moderate surface variation.

Standard is the safest starting point.

---

#### **Billow**

Billow uses absolute noise behavior, producing softer rounded variation.

It can make rock forms feel puffier or more worn. It can work for eroded boulders, weathered limestone, or rounded volcanic forms, but it can also make rocks look inflated if pushed too far.

Use Billow when Ridged feels too sharp and Standard feels too uneven.

---

#### **Ridged**

Ridged emphasizes ridge-like transitions.

It is useful for sharper creases, folded surfaces, fractured edges, shale-like pieces, and more aggressive stone breakup.

Good for:

* Metamorphic-looking folds.  
* Shale slabs.  
* Craggy surfaces.  
* Cliff fragments.  
* Layered or compressed forms.

Too much Ridged detail can make the surface look scratchy or over-carved.

---

#### **PingPong**

PingPong creates folded, oscillating noise.

It can produce stylized surface rhythm and repeated ridges. It is less universally natural than Standard or Ridged, but it can be useful for fantasy rocks, mineral forms, or deliberately unusual surfaces.

Use it carefully. It can become pattern-like very quickly.

---

#### **Swiss Erosion**

Swiss Erosion creates stronger eroded ridge behavior.

It can be useful for sweeping cliffs, alpine spires, volcanic fragments, and harder rock forms where you want more directional erosion-like breakup.

This is not a real erosion simulation. It is a useful procedural noise style that can suggest eroded rock.

If it becomes too harsh, reduce displacement strength before changing every other control.

---

### **Octaves**

Octaves control how many layers of noise are combined.

More octaves add complexity. They can make a rock feel less smooth and less computer-generated. But more is not always better.

Use fewer octaves for:

* Smooth river stones.  
* Obsidian-like pieces.  
* Polished mineral chunks.  
* Stylized low-detail rocks.  
* Mobile-friendly assets.

Use more octaves for:

* Rough sandstone.  
* Broken granite.  
* Pumice.  
* Cliff fragments.  
* Weathered boulders.

If the rock starts to look like static or crumbly foam, reduce octaves, persistence, or displacement strength.

---

### **Lacunarity**

Lacunarity controls how quickly noise detail changes size from one octave to the next.

A value around 2 is a typical starting point.

Higher values create more separation between large and small detail. This can make the rock feel sharper or more chaotic.

If Lacunarity is too high, detail can become harsh, scratchy, or noisy. If it is too low, the detail layers may feel too similar and muddy.

For most rocks, adjust Displacement Strength and Detail Scale before changing Lacunarity heavily.

---

### **Persistence**

Persistence controls how strongly the smaller octave layers contribute.

Higher persistence makes small details more visible.

Lower persistence keeps the surface cleaner and lets the large forms dominate.

Use higher persistence for rough materials such as pumice, chalk, weathered sandstone, or broken volcanic rock.

Use lower persistence for smoother boulders, polished minerals, marble-like pieces, obsidian, or stylized assets.

If the rock looks grainy or noisy in geometry, reduce Persistence before reducing all detail noise.

---

### **Domain Warping**

Domain Warping bends the sampling space before detail noise is evaluated.

In practical terms, it twists or flows the detail pattern.

Domain Warping settings include:

**Warp Strength**  
 Controls how strongly the sampling space is distorted.

**Warp Scale**  
 Controls the size of the warp pattern. Larger values create broader flowing changes. Smaller values create tighter knots and twists.

Domain Warping is useful for:

* Folded metamorphic stone.  
* Schist-like shapes.  
* Marble-like swirling.  
* Stretched or compressed rock.  
* Less uniform detail breakup.  
* Rocks that should not look evenly noisy.

Domain Warping is usually not the first setting to adjust. Use it when the rock already has a good base shape, but the detail feels too evenly distributed.

If Warp Strength is too high, the rock may look melted, twisted, cloth-like, or liquid. If Warp Scale is too small, the warping can turn into noisy knots rather than readable folding.

---

### **Voronoi**

Voronoi adds cellular or distance-field patterns into the detail noise. It can create cracks, facets, pores, cells, crystalline surfaces, or stylized fracture patterns depending on the output type, metric, scale, and intensity.

Voronoi settings include:

**Pattern Output**  
 Controls which Voronoi value is used.

**Distance Metric**  
 Controls the shape of the cells.

**Voronoi Scale**  
 Controls the size of the cells, cracks, or facets.

**Blend Intensity**  
 Controls how strongly Voronoi replaces or blends into the detail noise.

Voronoi is useful for:

* Cracked volcanic rock.  
* Columnar basalt-like fragments.  
* Pumice pores.  
* Meteorite-like surfaces.  
* Mineral nodules.  
* Crystalline or fantasy rocks.  
* Stylized fracture patterns.

Voronoi is also easy to overuse.

If the rock starts looking like sci-fi armor plating, melted foam, leopard spots, or a procedural test material, reduce Voronoi Blend Intensity first. If the cells are too small, increase Voronoi Scale. If the cells are too large, decrease Voronoi Scale.

---

### **Voronoi Pattern Output**

Lite supports these Voronoi output styles:

---

#### **F1**

F1 uses the distance to the nearest cell point.

This often creates cell-like or blob-like structure. It can be useful for pitted surfaces, nodules, clustered minerals, and some stylized rock breakup.

---

#### **F2**

F2 uses the distance to the second-nearest cell point.

This can produce broader cellular variation. It is usually less crack-like than F2 Minus F1 or Edge.

Use it when you want uneven cellular forms without very sharp crack lines.

---

#### **F2 Minus F1**

F2 Minus F1 emphasizes the difference between the nearest and second-nearest cell distances.

This is often useful for crack-like or vein-like structure. Igneous Rock Type uses this output because it tends to create a stronger fractured read.

Use it for volcanic fragments, cracked stone, fractured minerals, or stylized crystalline surfaces.

---

#### **Edge**

Edge focuses more directly on cell boundaries.

Use it when you want stronger crack or border behavior.

Edge can become artificial quickly, especially at high intensity or small scale. It is useful for game-readable cracks, but it should be tuned carefully if the rock is supposed to look natural.

---

### **Voronoi Distance Metric**

Lite supports three Voronoi distance metrics:

---

#### **Euclidean**

Euclidean creates more rounded, organic cell shapes.

Use it for natural cracks, pores, nodules, and softer cellular breakup.

---

#### **Manhattan**

Manhattan creates more diamond-like or taxicab-distance patterns.

Use it for stylized rocks, unusual minerals, or more angular breakup.

---

#### **Chebyshev**

Chebyshev creates more square or block-like cells.

Use it for geometric minerals, bismuth-like stylization, blocky basalt, or deliberately artificial crystalline forms.

Chebyshev can look very game-like, which may be useful. It is usually less subtle than Euclidean.

---

### **Terracing**

Terracing quantizes the displacement into stepped layers.

It is useful for sedimentary rocks, layered cliffs, eroded ledges, shale slabs, canyon walls, limestone terraces, and stylized stratified formations.

Terracing settings include:

**Terrace Count**  
 Controls how many steps are generated.

**Terrace Intensity**  
 Controls how strongly the steps are applied.

Higher Terrace Intensity creates clearer ledges. Lower intensity creates softer layering.

Terracing is often paired with the Sedimentary Strata color pattern. The shape creates ledges, while the color pattern creates horizontal bands. Together, they can make sandstone, shale, or canyon rock read more clearly.

If terracing is too strong, the rock may look like stairs, a stepped low-poly mesh, or carved foam. If terracing is too weak, the layers may only be visible in color and not in the mesh shape.

---

### **Working Safely With Shape Settings**

Procedural shape controls interact strongly. A rock can go from useful to unusable with only a few slider changes.

For beginner use:

1. Start from a Quick Start Preset.  
2. Change the seed.  
3. Adjust Prefab Scale.  
4. Adjust proportions.  
5. Adjust Macro Noise only if the broad silhouette needs it.  
6. Adjust Detail Noise only after the large shape works.  
7. Add Domain Warping, Voronoi, or Terracing only when the style needs it.

For production use:

1. Make a saved RockSettings profile before heavy edits.  
2. Duplicate or Save As when branching a style.  
3. Check the rock at multiple LODs.  
4. Avoid relying on a single high-detail LOD to hide a bad shape.  
5. Reuse good profiles and generate seed variations from them.  
6. Use Pro when you need batch generation or larger-scale profile management.

---

### **Common Shape Problems**

**The rock looks like a sphere with noise on it.**  
 Increase macro displacement, adjust proportions, or choose a more appropriate base shape.

**The rock looks melted.**  
 Reduce Domain Warp Strength, Macro Noise Strength, or Detail Noise Strength.

**The rock looks too spiky.**  
 Reduce Detail Noise Strength, reduce Persistence, or use a broader Detail Scale.

**The rock looks like foam or cellular plastic.**  
 Reduce Voronoi Blend Intensity or increase Voronoi Scale.

**The rock looks like stairs.**  
 Reduce Terrace Intensity or increase Terrace Count.

**The silhouette is good, but the surface looks flat.**  
 Do not over-adjust geometry. Move to texturing, procedural bump, and micro-detail.

**The surface looks detailed up close but messy from far away.**  
 Check lower LODs. Detail that works on LOD 0 may collapse visually at lower mesh densities.

**The rock looks worse after several edits.**  
 Go back to the preset, change one group of settings at a time, and save useful intermediate profiles.

---

### **Lite Shape Limits and Pro Upgrade Notes**

Rock Generator Lite includes the core individual-rock shape workflow. It is enough to make useful standalone rocks, test the generator, and build smaller rock sets.

Rock Generator Pro is the recommended upgrade when you need:

* More noise methods.  
* More generation methods.  
* Batch rock generation.  
* A broader profile workflow.  
* More production-oriented rock set management.  
* A stronger placement workflow.  
* Runtime MonoBehaviour tools and a fuller runtime-oriented setup.

Lite can make good rocks, but Pro is the version intended for larger and more serious environment production.

## **6\. Procedural Texturing and Texture Baking**

Rock Generator Lite can color generated rocks using vertex colors or baked textures. The texturing controls are found in **Procedural Texturing & Bump**.

This is one of the most important parts of the asset, and it is also one of the easiest parts to misuse.

The texturing system is layered. Color pattern, cavity color, slope placement, strata bands, organic patches, triplanar input textures, procedural bump, micro-detail, AO, height, smoothness, and metallic deposits can all affect the final result.

Because of that, randomly sliding texturing controls is not usually a good workflow. It can produce muddy colors, overly sharp bands, noisy bump maps, plastic smoothness, or mineral effects that look more like paint than stone.

A better workflow is:

1. Start from a preset.  
2. Make sure the shape reads well.  
3. Adjust the main colors.  
4. Adjust cavity color and cavity strength.  
5. Adjust pattern scale or band scale.  
6. Add bump after color is working.  
7. Add micro-detail only when the surface needs fine grain.  
8. Use metallic/mineral settings only when the material style needs them.

Rock Generator Pro is the better choice when you need higher texture resolutions, more texture inputs, richer layered texturing, texture splatting on strata layers, batch workflows, or atlas combining.

---

### **Output Texturing Method**

The **Output Texturing Method** controls how the generated rock receives its final color and material data.

Rock Generator Lite includes three methods:

* Vertex Colors  
* Procedural Texture Bake  
* Triplanar Input Bake

---

### **Vertex Colors**

Vertex Colors stores procedural color data in the mesh vertex colors.

This avoids generated texture files. It can be useful for:

* Mobile-style rocks.  
* Lightweight assets.  
* Stylized low-resource scenes.  
* Runtime-oriented experiments.  
* Projects using a custom vertex-color shader.

Vertex Color mode is not the best-looking mode in Lite. It is lower fidelity than baked texture output because color detail depends on mesh density and shader support. It also does not use baked albedo, normal, mask, AO, height, smoothness, or metallic texture output in the same way baked modes do.

The included presets are primarily authored for baked texture output. There are no dedicated Vertex Color presets. You can apply an existing preset and switch it to Vertex Colors as an approximation, but expect a simpler result.

For Vertex Color mode to display correctly, the material or shader must read mesh vertex colors. If the shader does not read vertex colors, the rock may appear plain, white, gray, or incorrectly colored.

Use Vertex Colors when performance simplicity matters more than material richness. Use Procedural Texture Bake when visual quality matters more.

---

### **Procedural Texture Bake**

Procedural Texture Bake generates texture maps from the procedural color, cavity, normal, metallic, smoothness, and related settings.

This is the standard baked workflow in Lite and is the recommended mode for most rocks.

Use Procedural Texture Bake when you want:

* Better color detail.  
* Baked normal maps.  
* Procedural bump.  
* Micro-detail.  
* AO maps.  
* Height maps.  
* Smoothness maps.  
* Metallic or mineral effects.  
* More conventional Unity material output.

Most included presets are intended to be viewed in baked texture mode. Mineral, metallic, strata, pumice, granite, marble, obsidian, and lichen-style presets usually read better with baked output.

---

### **Triplanar Input Bake**

Triplanar Input Bake uses assigned input albedo and normal textures, projects them across the generated rock, combines them with procedural overlays, and bakes the result into texture maps.

Use this when you already have tiling rock textures and want them baked onto the generated mesh.

This can be useful for:

* Matching rocks to an existing terrain material.  
* Using a studio’s own tiling rock textures.  
* Baking a consistent material style into generated prefabs.  
* Reducing visible UV seams through triplanar projection.

Lite supports a limited custom texture input workflow: one input albedo and one input normal, plus procedural overlays.

Rock Generator Pro is the better choice if you need more texture inputs, richer blending, texture splatting, separate texture treatment for strata layers, or a broader material authoring workflow.

---

### **Bake Resolution**

The **Bake Resolution (Albedo)** setting controls the main baked texture size.

Rock Generator Lite supports baked textures up to:

**1024×1024**

The interface may show 2048 and 4096 as Pro options, but those are not available in Lite. Selecting those options in Lite shows a Pro notice and returns the bake size to 1024\. The baker also clamps resolution internally.

Use lower resolutions while testing. Use 1024 when the rock is close to final and you need to inspect the baked material more accurately.

Rock Generator Pro supports higher-resolution baking and is the recommended version when you need 2K or 4K textures.

---

### **Map Resolution Scaling**

The normal map and auxiliary maps can be generated at reduced resolution relative to the albedo map.

**Normal Map Scale** controls the baked normal map resolution.

**Auxiliary Maps Scale** controls maps such as mask, metallic, AO, smoothness, and height outputs.

For example, you can use a 1024 albedo while generating auxiliary maps at half or quarter resolution to reduce file size.

Use full resolution when:

* The rock will be seen close to the camera.  
* Normal detail is important.  
* Metallic or smoothness detail needs to be sharp.  
* The asset is a hero prop.

Use lower auxiliary resolutions when:

* The rock is background scatter.  
* The material is simple.  
* You are building a small reusable set.  
* Project size matters more than fine material detail.

---

### **Color Pattern Style**

The **Color Pattern Style** controls how procedural colors are distributed.

Rock Generator Lite includes three color patterns:

* Slope & Cavity  
* Sedimentary Strata  
* Organic Patches

These are game-art patterns. They are not physical weathering or geological simulation systems.

---

### **Slope & Cavity**

Slope & Cavity blends between a primary base color, a secondary slope color, and a cavity color.

Use it for:

* General boulders.  
* Mossy rocks.  
* Snow or frost on upper surfaces.  
* Dusty ledges.  
* Darker undersides.  
* Volcanic rock with subtle surface variation.  
* Wet/dry surface separation.  
* General color breakup.

Related settings include:

**Primary Color**  
 The base color of the rock.

**Secondary Color**  
 The color placed by slope rules.

**Slope Mode**  
 Controls whether slope placement is disabled, upward-facing only, or upward/downward.

**Spread Amount**  
 Controls how far the slope color spreads.

**Blend Smoothness**  
 Controls how soft the transition is.

**Pattern Scale**  
 Controls the size of the breakup pattern.

**Pattern Breakup Intensity**  
 Controls how much texturing noise interrupts the slope blend.

**Cavity Color**  
 The color used in crevices and high-detail areas.

**Cavity Shadow Intensity**  
 Controls how strongly cavity color is applied.

---

### **Slope Mode**

Slope Mode controls where the secondary color appears.

---

#### **None**

Disables slope-based color placement.

Use this when you want a simpler two-tone or cavity-only rock.

---

#### **Upward Only**

Places the secondary color on upward-facing surfaces.

This is useful for:

* Moss on top of stones.  
* Snow or frost on ledges.  
* Dust on upper surfaces.  
* Sun-bleached upper faces.  
* Dry top surfaces.

If Spread Amount is too high, the secondary color can cover too much of the rock and look painted on.

---

#### **Upward and Downward**

Uses both upward and downward-facing surface direction.

This can create stronger contrast across the rock and can help stylized or mineral presets. It is also useful when you want the secondary color to appear on both exposed faces and underside-like faces.

Use it carefully for natural rocks. It can look artificial if the colors are too different.

---

### **Cavity Color**

Cavity Color is applied to crevices and areas with stronger procedural surface detail.

This is useful for:

* Dirt in cracks.  
* Shadowed crevices.  
* Wet-looking recesses.  
* Oxidized seams.  
* Dark mineral pockets.  
* Moss or lichen caught in rough surfaces.

Cavity Shadow Intensity controls how strongly the cavity color appears.

If cavity strength is too low, the rock can look flat and evenly colored.

If cavity strength is too high, the rock may look dirty, burned, or outlined with artificial dark lines.

Cavity color is a visual approximation. It is not a simulated dirt, moss, water, or weathering system.

---

### **Sedimentary Strata**

Sedimentary Strata creates horizontal banding based on the generated rock position.

Use it for:

* Sandstone.  
* Shale.  
* Canyon rocks.  
* Layered cliff chunks.  
* Limestone terraces.  
* Stylized sedimentary slabs.  
* Some marble-like banded looks.

Related settings include:

**Base Strata Color**  
 The first band color.

**Mid Strata Color**  
 The second band color.

**Dark Strata Color**  
 The third band color.

**Strata Band Scale**  
 Controls the size and spacing of the bands.

**Strata Roughness**  
 Controls how much noise breaks up the banding.

**Warp Scale**  
 Controls the size of the wavy band distortion.

**Warp Strength**  
 Controls how strongly the bands are warped.

**Cavity Color**  
 Adds darker or contrasting color into crevices.

**Cavity Shadow Intensity**  
 Controls how strongly cavity color appears.

---

### **Using Strata Well**

Sedimentary Strata works best when paired with shapes that support layering.

Good pairings:

* CubeSphere base shape.  
* Flattened or slab-like proportions.  
* Terracing enabled.  
* Moderate macro displacement.  
* Low to moderate detail displacement.  
* Warm sandstone, gray shale, chalk, or limestone colors.

Strata does not need to be perfectly horizontal to look useful. Slight warp often makes it more natural. Real sedimentary rock is rarely a stack of perfectly straight colored lines once it has been fractured, weathered, or eroded.

However, too much warp can make sedimentary bands look like melted candy or marble rather than sedimentary layering.

If the bands look too clean, increase Strata Roughness slightly.  
 If the bands look noisy or muddy, reduce Strata Roughness.  
 If the bands are too thin, increase Strata Band Scale.  
 If the bands are too broad, decrease Strata Band Scale.

Lite strata uses procedural color bands. Pro is the better choice when you want richer texture-based strata workflows, including separate texture splatting or blending for layers rather than only color-band control.

---

### **Organic Patches**

Organic Patches creates patch-like color variation using procedural noise.

Use it for:

* Mossy stone.  
* Lichen.  
* Granite speckling.  
* Limestone variation.  
* Rhyolite-like color patches.  
* Oxidation.  
* Mineral staining.  
* Weathered surface breakup.

Related settings include:

**Base Rock Color**  
 The main material color.

**Primary Patch Color**  
 The first patch color.

**Secondary Patch Color**  
 The second patch color.

**Patch Scale**  
 Controls the size of the patch regions.

**Noise Breakup Scale**  
 Controls the noise used to break patch edges.

**Edge Breakup Intensity**  
 Controls how rough or soft the patch boundaries are.

**Cavity Color**  
 Adds color in crevices.

**Cavity Shadow Intensity**  
 Controls how strongly cavity color appears.

---

### **Using Organic Patches Well**

Organic Patches is useful when a rock should not be evenly colored.

It can suggest biological growth, staining, mineral variation, or speckled composition. For example, granite-like presets often depend on small patches and strong color contrast. Mossy or lichen-covered rocks often use larger, softer patches.

If Patch Scale is too small, the rock may look speckled or noisy. This can be useful for granite but poor for moss.

If Patch Scale is too large, the rock may look like camouflage or large painted blobs.

If Edge Breakup Intensity is too high, the color placement may become noisy and muddy. If it is too low, patch edges may look too clean.

Use Organic Patches when you want varied surface color, not when you need clear geological layering. Use Sedimentary Strata for readable layers.

---

### **Triplanar Input Textures**

When using **Triplanar Input Bake**, the tool exposes input texture settings.

**Albedo Map (Tiled)**  
 The input color texture projected across the rock.

**Normal Map (Tiled)**  
 The input normal texture projected across the rock.

**Triplanar Tiling Scale**  
 Controls how often the input textures repeat across the rock.

This is different from many of the procedural “Scale” controls. Higher Triplanar Tiling Scale values make the input texture repeat more often, so details appear smaller.

Use lower tiling for large, broad texture features. Use higher tiling for finer material grain.

The procedural overlays still matter. Cavity color, slope placement, and other procedural controls can modify the triplanar result.

Lite supports a small number of custom texture inputs. Pro is the better version when you need more texture inputs, more advanced blending, or larger material authoring workflows.

---

### **Procedural Bump Map**

When using a baked texture mode, **Enable Procedural Bump Map** adds procedural normal detail to the baked normal map.

This affects lighting. It does not change the mesh silhouette.

Related settings include:

**Bump Scale**  
 Controls the size of the bump pattern.

**Bump Strength**  
 Controls how strongly the bump affects the normal map.

**Baked Normal Strength**  
 Controls the final normal map intensity.

Bump is useful for most baked rocks. It helps surfaces catch light and makes a rock feel less flat without increasing mesh density.

Use bump for:

* Sandstone grain.  
* Pumice pores.  
* Chalky surfaces.  
* Weathered limestone.  
* Rough granite.  
* Volcanic fragments.  
* Dry cracked stone.  
* General surface roughness.

Do not use bump as a replacement for geometry when the silhouette is wrong. If the outline needs cracks, ledges, or large chips, use shape noise, terracing, Voronoi, or proportions instead.

---

### **Recommended Bump Workflow**

Use bump after the shape and color already work.

A safe workflow is:

1. Set the rock shape first.  
2. Set the main color pattern.  
3. Add cavity color.  
4. Enable procedural bump.  
5. Keep Baked Normal Strength around 1.0 for most rocks.  
6. Tune Bump Scale and Bump Strength moderately.  
7. Only then consider Micro Detail.

The older bump-scale issue warned about in earlier tooltip language has been largely addressed by diameter-relative scale handling. Bump and micro-detail scales are now constrained relative to the generated rock diameter.

That does not mean bump always looks good. It is still easy to overdo.

If bump is too strong, the rock can look crunchy, noisy, glittery, or artificially sharpened.

If bump scale is too small, the surface may shimmer or look like fine static.

If bump scale is too large, the normal map may fight the mesh shape and look like broad dents painted onto the surface.

If Baked Normal Strength is too high, lighting artifacts become more visible.

For most rocks, moderate bump is better than extreme bump.

---

### **Micro Detail**

**Add Micro-Detail Bump** adds an extra high-frequency normal layer.

Micro Detail is for fine surface texture only. It does not change geometry.

Use it for:

* Sandstone grain.  
* Chalk dust.  
* Mudstone roughness.  
* Pumice pores.  
* Weathered limestone.  
* Fine mineral texture.  
* Subtle gritty surfaces.

Micro Detail should usually be subtle. It is best used after the main bump map is already behaving well.

If Micro Detail is too strong, the rock may look noisy rather than detailed. It can also create specular flicker under strong lighting, especially if smoothness is high.

If the silhouette is still too smooth, do not use Micro Detail to fix it. Use geometry noise instead.

For most rocks:

* Use bump more often than micro-detail.  
* Use micro-detail only when the material needs fine grit.  
* Keep Micro Strength low unless the preset is intentionally rough or porous.  
* Reduce smoothness if micro-detail makes the surface sparkle.

---

### **Ambient Occlusion**

**Generate AO Map** creates an ambient occlusion-style map based on procedural cavity and surface information.

AO helps crevices feel deeper and can make baked rocks read better in a scene.

Use AO for:

* Craggy rocks.  
* Rocks with strong cavities.  
* Pumice.  
* Shale.  
* Cliff chunks.  
* Mineral pockets.  
* Dark volcanic surfaces.

If AO Strength is too high, the rock may look dirty or overly shadowed. If it is too low, the material may look flat.

AO is a baked approximation. It is not a replacement for scene lighting or real contact shadows.

---

### **Height Map**

**Generate Height Map** saves a separate height texture when enabled.

In Lite, height is **not** packed into the blue channel of the mask map. The packed mask map blue channel is unused/zero. Height is saved as its own texture when height generation is enabled.

Use height maps when your shader or render pipeline material can use them. If your shader does not use height/parallax data, generating the height texture may not visibly affect the material until you assign it to a shader that supports it.

Height maps can be useful for:

* Parallax-style material setups.  
* Custom shaders.  
* Additional material authoring.  
* Exporting generated texture data for another workflow.

If you do not use height in your material, disable it to reduce generated texture output.

---

### **Smoothness**

**Generate Smoothness Map** creates smoothness variation.

**Base Smoothness** controls the general smoothness of the rock.

Most dry stone should use low smoothness. Values around the lower range usually look more natural for dry rock.

Higher smoothness can be useful for:

* Wet stone.  
* Obsidian.  
* Polished minerals.  
* Quartz.  
* Marble.  
* Metallic deposits.  
* Stylized fantasy materials.

If smoothness is too high, the rock can look like plastic, wet glass, polished resin, or toy material. This becomes more obvious when bump or micro-detail is also strong.

If the rock looks too shiny, reduce Base Smoothness first.

---

### **Metals and Minerals**

The **Metals & Minerals** settings are available in baked texture modes.

Lite includes three metallic styles:

* Veins  
* Cavity Deposits  
* Crystalline Nodules

These settings affect baked albedo, metallic, and smoothness outputs. They do not create separate physical ore geometry or crystal extrusions in Lite.

Metallic effects are partly stylized. In real rock, metal-bearing minerals may be subtle, weathered, oxidized, mixed into the stone, or visible only in small traces. For game use, it is often helpful to exaggerate veins or deposits so the material reads clearly.

That is especially true for gold, pyrite, copper, lapis-like materials, hematite, and fantasy geode styles. A game-readable gold vein may not be a perfectly realistic geological presentation, but it can be useful in an environment or collectible resource context.

Rock Generator Pro is the better choice if you need more advanced mineral, crystal, texture-input, or layered material workflows.

---

### **Metallic Style: Veins**

**Veins** creates vein-like metallic deposits.

Use it for:

* Gold-veined quartz.  
* Pyrite streaks.  
* Copper-bearing cracks.  
* Stylized ore rocks.  
* Fantasy resource nodes.

Veins work best when the rock has enough contrast between base color and ore color.

If Ore Coverage is too high, veins can cover the rock and look painted on.

If Ore Scale is too small, the veins may become noisy. If it is too large, the rock may only get one or two broad metallic shapes.

Use baked texture output for metallic presets. Vertex Color mode will not reproduce the same material behavior.

---

### **Metallic Style: Cavity Deposits**

**Cavity Deposits** places metallic or mineral material more strongly in cavity-style regions.

Use it for:

* Iron-rich pockets.  
* Hydrothermal vent deposits.  
* Weathered ore in cracks.  
* Dark metallic stains.  
* Mineralized crevices.

This style usually works best on rocks with visible roughness, cavities, or darker recesses.

If coverage is too high, the rock may look dirty or uniformly metallic. If it is too low, deposits may be hard to see.

---

### **Metallic Style: Crystalline Nodules**

**Crystalline Nodules** creates clustered mineral-like deposits.

Use it for:

* Lapis and pyrite-style rocks.  
* Fantasy geodes.  
* Mineral clusters.  
* Stylized crystalline materials.  
* Resource-node props.

This is a surface/material effect in Lite. It does not create separate crystal meshes.

If you need physical crystal protrusions or more advanced mineral forms, use Rock Generator Pro.

---

### **Ore Settings**

Metallic styles share several settings:

**Ore Color**  
 The color of the metallic or mineral deposit.

**Ore Scale**  
 Controls the size of the ore pattern.

**Ore Coverage**  
 Controls how much of the rock receives ore.

**Ore Metallic**  
 Controls how metallic the ore is.

**Ore Smoothness**  
 Controls how smooth or polished the ore appears.

For most believable rocks, avoid pushing Ore Coverage and Ore Smoothness too high together. Large areas of high-metallic, high-smoothness material can make the whole rock look artificial unless it is intentionally a fantasy or sci-fi prop.

---

### **Texture Export Mode**

The **Texture Export Mode** controls how auxiliary texture data is saved.

Lite supports:

* Packed Mask Map  
* Individual Maps

---

### **Packed Mask Map**

Packed Mask Map stores multiple material channels in one texture.

In Lite, the packed mask map layout is:

**Red:** Metallic  
 **Green:** Ambient Occlusion  
 **Blue:** Unused / zero  
 **Alpha:** Smoothness

Height is not packed into the blue channel. If height generation is enabled, Lite saves height as a separate height texture.

Packed output is useful when your shader or render pipeline expects packed material data. It can also reduce the number of separate texture files.

---

### **Individual Maps**

Individual Maps saves separate grayscale textures for available auxiliary data.

Depending on settings, Lite can save separate maps such as:

* Metallic  
* Ambient Occlusion  
* Smoothness  
* Height

Use Individual Maps when your shader setup expects separate textures or when you want easier manual inspection/editing of each channel.

Individual maps are easier to understand but can create more files.

---

### **Generated Texture Outputs**

Depending on the selected settings, Lite can save:

* Albedo texture.  
* Normal texture.  
* Packed mask map.  
* Metallic texture.  
* Ambient occlusion texture.  
* Height texture.  
* Smoothness texture.

The exact output depends on:

* Output Texturing Method.  
* Texture Export Mode.  
* AO generation.  
* Height generation.  
* Smoothness generation.  
* Metallic style.  
* Normal map and auxiliary map resolution scale.

Baked texture output creates saved project assets. Keep generated rocks in organized folders so test bakes do not become difficult to clean up.

---

### **Preview Textures vs Saved Textures**

The Rock Window can create temporary preview textures so you can inspect the material before saving.

These preview textures exist only in memory. They are not the final generated texture assets.

Final textures are written to disk when you use **Generate Prefab (Save to Project)**.

Use **Force Update Colors** when the preview does not seem to reflect recent color, bump, micro-detail, AO, metallic, or smoothness changes.

Use **Refresh** if the preview appears visually corrupted rather than merely out of date.

---

### **Compute Shader Requirement**

Texture baking uses the **RockBakerLite.compute** compute shader.

If the compute shader is missing or the platform does not support Compute Shaders, baked texture generation will fail.

Mesh generation can still exist separately, but the baked texture workflow depends on compute shader support.

If texture baking fails, check:

* Compute Shader support on the target platform.  
* That RockBakerLite.compute exists in the project.  
* That the selected graphics API supports the compute workflow.  
* That the selected bake resolution is within Lite limits.  
* That the generated material is compatible with your render pipeline.

---

### **Vertex Color Caveats**

Vertex Color mode is useful, but it should be understood clearly.

It is best for lightweight/mobile-style output or custom shaders. It is not the visual target for most included presets.

Limitations:

* No baked albedo texture.  
* No baked normal map texture.  
* No baked mask map workflow.  
* Lower color fidelity.  
* Depends on mesh density.  
* Requires a shader that displays vertex colors.  
* Metallic/mineral presets will not read the same way as baked versions.

A practical workflow is to apply a preset, switch to Vertex Colors, then adjust colors and material/shader setup manually. Treat it as an approximation, not as a one-click equivalent to the baked preset.

---

### **Texturing Workflow Advice**

For beginner use:

1. Start with Procedural Texture Bake.  
2. Use a Quick Start Preset.  
3. Change the seed.  
4. Adjust main colors.  
5. Adjust cavity color.  
6. Use Force Update Colors to preview.  
7. Add bump carefully.  
8. Avoid changing every material setting at once.

For production use:

1. Save useful RockSettings profiles.  
2. Generate a small set of reusable variants.  
3. Avoid making every placed rock a unique material unless needed.  
4. Use lower map resolutions for background rocks.  
5. Use full 1024 only when the rock will be seen close up.  
6. Move to Pro when you need higher resolution, more texture inputs, texture splatting, batch generation, or atlas combining.

---

### **Common Texturing Problems**

**The rock looks muddy.**  
 Reduce cavity strength, reduce texturing noise blend, or choose colors with clearer separation.

**The rock looks too shiny.**  
 Lower Base Smoothness. Dry stone usually needs low smoothness.

**The bump looks noisy or glittery.**  
 Lower Bump Strength, increase Bump Scale, lower Baked Normal Strength, or reduce Micro Strength.

**The rock looks like plastic.**  
 Lower smoothness and reduce excessive normal strength.

**The strata bands look too perfect.**  
 Add a small amount of warp or roughness.

**The strata bands look melted.**  
 Reduce Warp Strength.

**The patches look like camouflage.**  
 Change Patch Scale, reduce Edge Breakup Intensity, or use subtler colors.

**The ore looks painted on.**  
 Reduce Ore Coverage, adjust Ore Scale, or reduce contrast between Ore Color and base color.

**The metallic areas dominate the whole rock.**  
 Lower Ore Coverage and Ore Metallic.

**The rock looks uncolored in Vertex Color mode.**  
 Use a shader/material that reads vertex colors.

**The height map does not seem to do anything.**  
 Make sure your material/shader actually uses the generated height texture.

---

### **Lite Texture Limits and Pro Upgrade Notes**

Rock Generator Lite is suitable for learning the texturing workflow and creating individual baked rock assets.

Its main texture limits are:

* Texture baking is capped at 1024×1024.  
* Texture input support is limited.  
* Strata layers are color-based in Lite.  
* Large numbers of unique baked materials can become hard to manage manually.  
* Runtime texture baking is not part of the Lite workflow.  
* The material workflow is narrower than Pro.

Rock Generator Pro is the recommended upgrade when you need:

* 2K or 4K texture baking.  
* More texture inputs.  
* More texturing methods.  
* Texture splatting or richer texture treatment for layered materials.  
* Batch rock generation.  
* A material and texture atlas combiner.  
* Better support for larger production environments.

Lite gives you a practical free workflow for individual rocks. Pro is the version intended for larger rock libraries, richer material variation, and more serious production use.

## **7\. LODs, Colliders, and Prefab Output**

Rock Generator Lite can generate a saved Unity prefab with visual LODs, mesh assets, a material, baked textures when needed, and optional collision.

This section explains what gets created, how the LOD and collider settings affect the result, and what to check before saving a rock.

The main goal is to create a clean reusable prefab. After generation, the baked prefab can be used like a normal Unity environment asset. It does not need the Rock Window to remain open, and generated baked prefabs do not require Rock Generator Lite Editor MonoBehaviours to function after export.

---

### **What the Generated Prefab Can Contain**

Depending on the current settings, a generated rock folder can include:

* The generated prefab.  
* One mesh asset per visual LOD.  
* A generated material.  
* Baked texture maps.  
* A packed mask map or individual auxiliary maps.  
* A separate collider mesh when needed.  
* A Unity LODGroup on the prefab root.  
* Child GameObjects for the visual LODs.  
* Optional primitive, convex mesh, or exact mesh collision.

The final prefab is intended to be placed, duplicated, scattered, or organized like any other Unity prefab.

For baked texture modes, the material and textures are saved as project assets. For Vertex Color mode, the generator saves meshes and a material but does not create the same baked texture set.

---

### **LOD Settings**

The **LOD Settings** foldout controls the generated mesh levels for the final prefab.

Each LOD entry includes two important fields:

**Mesh Density**  
 The density field changes depending on the selected base shape.

**Screen Transition %**  
 Controls when Unity’s LODGroup switches to that LOD based on screen-relative object size.

The generated prefab uses these entries to create a Unity LODGroup. Each visual LOD is generated as its own mesh, then assigned to a child object under the prefab root.

---

### **LODs Are Procedurally Regenerated, Not Decimated**

Rock Generator Lite does not create LODs by taking the highest-detail mesh and running a general mesh-collapse decimator on it.

Instead, each LOD is generated from a lower-resolution version of the same procedural primitive. The rock shape settings are applied again at that lower resolution.

This matters because lower LODs may not preserve every small chip, ridge, terrace, pore, or Voronoi feature from LOD 0\. They should preserve the broad silhouette and main read of the rock, but fine surface breakup may simplify.

Check lower LODs before exporting important rocks.

This is especially important for:

* Thin shale slabs.  
* Tall basalt-like pillars.  
* Terraced sandstone chunks.  
* Sharp alpine spires.  
* Rocks with strong Voronoi fracture.  
* Rocks with aggressive detail displacement.  
* Rocks that will be viewed from several distances.

---

### **Mesh Density for CubeSphere**

For **CubeSphere**, mesh density is controlled by **Grid Resolution**.

Higher Grid Resolution creates more vertices on each cube face before the shape is rounded and displaced. This gives the noise more geometry to work with and produces a smoother silhouette.

Approximate visual triangle count for CubeSphere is:

**12 × Grid Resolution²**

Examples:

* Grid Resolution 5 is low-detail.  
* Grid Resolution 10 is moderate.  
* Grid Resolution 20 is higher-detail.  
* Grid Resolution 50 is very dense and should be used carefully.

CubeSphere is useful for blocky, slabby, cliff-like, or columnar rocks. It often works well with terracing and sedimentary shapes because its topology supports flatter faces and more structured forms.

---

### **Mesh Density for Icosphere**

For **Icosphere**, mesh density is controlled by **Subdivision Level**.

Higher subdivision levels increase triangle count quickly. This can make rounded boulders and mineral shapes look smoother, but it also increases mesh size.

Approximate visual triangle count for Icosphere is:

**20 × 4^Subdivision Level**

Examples:

* Subdivision 2 gives a low-detail boulder shape.  
* Subdivision 3 is a useful middle range.  
* Subdivision 4 is a good higher-detail starting point for close rocks.  
* Subdivision 5 or 6 should be used only when the extra detail is justified.

Icosphere is useful for rounded boulders, river stones, mineral nodules, pumice chunks, quartz masses, and other organic forms.

---

### **Screen Transition %**

**Screen Transition %** is Unity’s screen-relative LOD threshold.

Higher values mean the LOD is used while the object appears larger on screen. Lower values mean the LOD is used farther away or when the object appears smaller.

A typical setup is:

* LOD 0 at a higher transition value for close viewing.  
* LOD 1 at a mid transition value.  
* LOD 2 at a low transition value for distant viewing.

The exact distance depends on the object bounds, camera field of view, screen size, and how large the rock appears in the scene. Treat Screen Transition % as a visual tuning control rather than a fixed meter distance.

Do not set every LOD to similar transition values. That can make LOD switching unclear or cause a lower LOD to appear too early.

---

### **Recommended LOD Workflow**

For a normal rock:

1. Start with the default LOD setup.  
2. Inspect LOD 0 in the preview.  
3. Switch to LOD 1 and check the silhouette.  
4. Switch to the lowest LOD and check whether the rock still reads correctly.  
5. Reduce high-detail density if the mesh is heavier than needed.  
6. Increase lower LOD density only if the shape collapses too much.

A lower LOD does not need every surface detail. It needs to preserve the main mass, height, width, and silhouette.

If the lower LOD looks too different from the high-detail version, increase its density or reduce the most aggressive shape noise settings.

---

### **LODs and Baked Textures**

When using a baked texture mode, the baked material is shared across the visual LODs.

The highest-detail visual mesh is the main source used for texture baking. Lower LODs use the same material and texture set.

This is usually fine, but it means very aggressive simplification can make texture detail and mesh shape feel slightly mismatched at lower LODs. For example, a texture may still show small cracks or bumps that no longer exist in the simplified geometry.

For most rocks, this is acceptable because lower LODs are seen from farther away. For hero rocks or large cliff pieces, inspect the lower LODs more carefully.

---

### **Triangle Counts in the Preview**

The Rock Window preview shows approximate triangle counts for each LOD in the **View LOD** dropdown.

Use these counts as a practical guide before generating the prefab.

High triangle counts may be justified for:

* Close-up rocks.  
* Large hero boulders.  
* Walkable cliff chunks.  
* Rocks with strong silhouette detail.  
* Rocks intended for screenshots or cinematic use.

Lower triangle counts are better for:

* Background scatter.  
* Small debris.  
* Distant rocks.  
* Mobile-style scenes.  
* Large quantities of repeated props.

Do not use high density just because it looks slightly better in the preview. A rock that will be seen at a distance usually does not need dense geometry.

---

### **LODs and Shape Noise**

Shape noise depends on mesh density.

If the mesh is too low-density, fine displacement cannot appear clearly. Detail noise may look faceted, stepped, or collapsed.

If the mesh is very high-density, aggressive noise can create too much geometric roughness and increase mesh size unnecessarily.

Use this rule:

**Use geometry density for silhouette and real surface form.**  
 **Use baked bump and micro-detail for lighting detail.**

If the rock silhouette needs chips, ridges, ledges, or broken shape, increase mesh density or adjust geometry noise.

If the silhouette is already good but the surface looks flat, use procedural bump instead of adding more mesh density.

---

### **Generated Mesh Assets**

When a prefab is generated, Lite saves each visual LOD mesh as a project asset.

The saved mesh names are based on the generated rock folder name and LOD index.

For example, a generated rock may contain:

* RockName\_LOD0  
* RockName\_LOD1  
* RockName\_LOD2

If a convex mesh collider is requested, Lite may also save a separate lower-detail collider mesh.

These are normal Unity mesh assets. They are part of the generated output asset set and can be inspected in the Project window.

---

### **Physics and Collisions**

The **Physics & Collisions** foldout controls the collider setup on the generated prefab.

Rock Generator Lite supports these collider types:

* None  
* Primitive Box  
* Primitive Sphere  
* Convex Mesh  
* Exact Mesh

Collider choice affects both physical behavior and performance. Most rocks do not need exact collision. Choose the simplest collider that works for the intended use.

---

### **Collider Type: None**

**None** generates no collider.

Use this for:

* Background rocks.  
* Distant scatter.  
* Decorative formations.  
* Rocks the player will never touch.  
* Props used only for visual dressing.

This is the cheapest option.

If a rock does not need gameplay collision, do not add a collider just because the option exists.

---

### **Collider Type: Primitive Box**

**Primitive Box** adds a Box Collider based on the bounds of the highest-detail visual mesh.

Use this for:

* Background rocks with rough collision.  
* Simple blocking volumes.  
* Low-cost scatter.  
* Debris that does not need accurate contact shape.  
* Mobile or performance-sensitive scenes.

Box collision is fast, but it may feel too square for rounded boulders.

It is often acceptable for background props or small rocks where the exact contact surface is not important.

---

### **Collider Type: Primitive Sphere**

**Primitive Sphere** adds a Sphere Collider based on the bounds of the highest-detail visual mesh.

Use this for:

* Rounded boulders.  
* Pebbles.  
* River stones.  
* Simple physics props.  
* Rocks where a soft approximate collision shape is acceptable.

Sphere collision is fast and stable, but it is a poor fit for slabs, pillars, cliff chunks, and flat rocks.

---

### **Collider Type: Convex Mesh**

**Convex Mesh** generates and assigns a lower-detail convex mesh collider.

This is usually the safest general-purpose collider choice in Lite.

Use Convex Mesh for:

* Normal gameplay rocks.  
* Medium boulders.  
* Props that need physical contact.  
* Rocks that may interact with rigidbodies.  
* Scene dressing where primitive collision is too rough.

Convex Mesh gives a more rock-like collision shape than a primitive collider while avoiding the cost and restrictions of exact non-convex collision.

In Lite, the convex collider is generated as a simplified procedural mesh. It is not the same as using the highest-detail visual mesh for collision. This is intentional. Collision should usually be simpler than the visible mesh.

---

### **Collider Type: Exact Mesh**

**Exact Mesh** uses one of the generated visual LOD meshes as a non-convex MeshCollider.

Use Exact Mesh only for static detailed collision.

Good cases include:

* Large static boulders the player can climb on.  
* Cliff sections.  
* Walkable rock shelves.  
* Environment pieces where the collision surface needs to match the visible surface more closely.

Avoid Exact Mesh for ordinary scattered rocks, small debris, dynamic physics objects, or large numbers of repeated props.

Exact Mesh colliders are non-convex. They are more expensive than primitive or convex colliders and are not the right default for most rocks.

---

### **Mesh Source for Exact Mesh Colliders**

When Exact Mesh is selected, the **Mesh Source** field controls which LOD mesh is used as the collider.

Using LOD 0 as the collider is often unnecessary. It may create a detailed collision mesh that costs more than the gameplay benefit justifies.

A lower LOD is usually a better collider source because it preserves the broad shape while reducing collision complexity.

Recommended workflow:

1. Select Exact Mesh only when detailed static collision is truly needed.  
2. Start with a lower LOD as the Mesh Source.  
3. Test player movement or physics contact.  
4. Increase collider detail only if the lower LOD causes obvious problems.

For many rocks, Convex Mesh is a better choice.

---

### **Collider Choice Guide**

Use this as a practical starting point:

**No gameplay contact**  
 Use None.

**Cheap background collision**  
 Use Primitive Box or Primitive Sphere.

**General-purpose rock collision**  
 Use Convex Mesh.

**Detailed static walkable collision**  
 Use Exact Mesh with a lower LOD source first.

**Dynamic physics object**  
 Avoid Exact Mesh. Use Convex Mesh or a primitive collider.

**Large cliff or walkable formation**  
 Exact Mesh may be appropriate, but keep the collider LOD reasonable.

---

### **Colliders and Prefab Scale**

Collider dimensions are built to match the generated rock and its Prefab Scale.

Remember that **Target Diameter** controls the generated mesh size, while **Prefab Scale** scales the finished prefab/LOD children and collider setup.

For ordinary size variation, it is often better to keep the generated rock near its preset Target Diameter and use Prefab Scale or scene instance scale.

This keeps the procedural material relationships more consistent while still allowing size variation in the scene.

---

### **Material Output**

The generated material depends on the selected Output Texturing Method.

---

#### **Vertex Color Material**

If the rock uses Vertex Colors, Lite creates a material intended to display vertex color data.

No baked albedo, normal, mask, AO, height, metallic, or smoothness textures are generated in this mode.

Vertex Color mode is useful for lightweight output, but it depends on shader support. If the assigned material or shader does not read vertex colors, the rock may look plain or incorrectly colored.

Vertex Color mode is usually lower fidelity than baked texture output and is best treated as a lightweight/mobile-style option.

---

#### **Baked Texture Material**

If the rock uses Procedural Texture Bake or Triplanar Input Bake, Lite bakes textures, imports them into the project, creates a material, and assigns the generated textures where supported.

The material creation path uses common Unity shader options where available:

* HDRP Lit when HDRP is active and available.  
* URP Lit when available.  
* Built-in Standard shader fallback when available.

If your project uses a custom shader or a heavily customized render pipeline, you may need to adjust the generated material after export.

---

### **Baked Texture Outputs**

Depending on the selected texture settings, generated output can include:

* Albedo texture.  
* Normal texture.  
* Packed mask map.  
* Metallic texture.  
* Ambient Occlusion texture.  
* Height texture.  
* Smoothness texture.

The exact set of generated files depends on:

* Output Texturing Method.  
* Texture Export Mode.  
* AO generation.  
* Height generation.  
* Smoothness generation.  
* Metallic style.  
* Normal map resolution scale.  
* Auxiliary map resolution scale.

The Lite texture baker is capped at 1024×1024. Use Rock Generator Pro when you need 2K or 4K texture output.

---

### **Packed Mask Map Reminder**

When using Packed Mask Map mode, Lite uses this channel layout:

**Red:** Metallic  
 **Green:** Ambient Occlusion  
 **Blue:** Unused / zero  
 **Alpha:** Smoothness

Height is not packed into the blue channel. If height generation is enabled, Lite saves height as a separate height texture.

This is important when using custom shaders. Do not assume that blue contains height data.

---

### **Output Folder Behavior**

Generated rocks are saved inside the selected **Output Directory**.

If the selected output directory does not exist, Lite attempts to create it. If the path cannot be validated or created, the generator falls back to a default rock output folder.

The normal Rock Window output path and the Demo Orchestrator output path are separate.

The Demo Orchestrator defaults to:

**Assets/VeridianData/RockGenLite/Demo\_Assets**

The normal Rock Window output uses the profile’s selected Output Directory. Depending on the profile or default settings, this may be a general generated-rock folder such as:

**Assets/VeridianData/RockGenerator/Rocks**

Use dedicated folders for different purposes:

* Demo output for temporary tests.  
* Production output for rocks you intend to keep.  
* Separate folders for different biomes, material styles, or level areas.

Good folder organization matters because baked rocks can generate several assets per prefab.

---

### **Unique Generated Folders**

Each generated rock is saved into a unique folder.

The folder name is based on the Rock Type and Export Name. Unity’s asset path system is used to avoid overwriting an existing generated folder.

For example, if you generate several rocks with the same Export Name, Unity will create unique paths rather than replacing the previous output.

This is useful for testing, but it can create many folders quickly. Use **Undo Last Generation** or manual cleanup when test output is no longer needed.

---

### **Undo Last Generation**

After generating a prefab, the Rock Window may show **Undo Last Generation**.

This deletes the folder created for the most recently generated rock.

Use it for accidental test output.

This is not the same as Unity’s normal scene Undo. It deletes saved project files from disk and bypasses the Unity Undo stack.

Use it carefully, especially if you have moved additional files into the generated folder.

---

### **Clean Prefab Workflow**

A generated baked rock prefab is a normal Unity prefab.

A typical generated prefab contains:

* A root GameObject with an LODGroup.  
* Child GameObjects for LOD meshes.  
* MeshFilter and MeshRenderer components.  
* A generated material.  
* Optional collider components or collider child objects.  
* References to saved mesh and texture assets.

The generated prefab can be:

* Dragged into scenes.  
* Duplicated.  
* Used with the Rock Placer.  
* Used in prefabs or level chunks.  
* Included in builds as a normal Unity asset.  
* Organized with the rest of your environment art.

Generated baked prefabs do not need Rock Generator Lite Editor tools to function after export. The generator creates the asset; the saved prefab is then ordinary project content.

---

### **Generated Assets and Reuse**

Generated meshes, materials, textures, and prefabs are generated output assets. You may use, modify, sell, and redistribute generated rock output assets in your own projects, asset packs, rendered media, and commercial work.

That permission applies to generated output only. It does not apply to redistributing the Rock Generator Lite or Pro package files, source code, editor tools, compute shaders, documentation, demo scripts, or original package content.

For practical production use, it is usually better to generate a reusable set of rock prefabs rather than a unique baked rock for every placement in a scene.

For example:

1. Generate 5–10 sandstone variants.  
2. Reuse and scale those prefabs across the scene.  
3. Use the Rock Placer for scatter.  
4. Keep material count under control.  
5. Move to Pro when you need batch generation or atlas/material combining.

---

### **Lite Output Limits and Pro Upgrade Notes**

Rock Generator Lite is useful for generating individual rocks and smaller reusable sets.

Its main output limits are:

* No batch rock generation.  
* Texture baking capped at 1024×1024.  
* Fewer noise and texturing options than Pro.  
* Limited custom texture input workflow.  
* No material/texture atlas combiner in Lite.  
* Basic terrain scatter workflow.  
* Manual management of larger generated libraries.

Rock Generator Pro is the recommended upgrade when output management becomes a real part of production.

Use Pro when you need:

* Batch generation of rock sets.  
* Higher texture bake resolutions.  
* More procedural generation options.  
* More texturing options.  
* More custom texture inputs.  
* Material and texture atlas combining.  
* A stronger placement workflow.  
* Local brush placement.  
* Better support for larger environment production.

Lite can produce usable rocks. Pro is the version intended to make that workflow scale better.

---

## **8\. Rock Placer**

The Rock Placer is a basic terrain scattering tool included with Rock Generator Lite. It takes generated rock prefabs and places them across a Unity Terrain using simple placement rules such as weight, spawn count, scale range, slope limits, height limits, surface alignment, vertical offset, and clumping.

The Lite Rock Placer is useful for testing generated rocks and creating simple scatter layouts. It is not intended to be the full placement workflow for a production environment.

Rock Generator Pro includes a more capable placement workflow, including local brush placement and additional placement features. Use Pro when placement becomes a major part of your environment-building process.

---

### **Opening the Rock Placer**

Open the Rock Placer from the Unity menu:

**Tools \> Veridian \> Rock Generator Lite \> 3\. Rock Placer**

The Rock Placer can also be opened from:

* The Rock Window.  
* The Demo Orchestrator after baking a demo rock.

When opened normally, the tool tries to find a Terrain in the current scene. If no Terrain is found, assign one manually in the Target Environment section.

---

### **What the Rock Placer Is For**

Use the Lite Rock Placer when you want to:

* Test generated rock prefabs on terrain.  
* Scatter a small set of rock variants.  
* Create simple rock coverage in a scene.  
* Check scale, slope, and alignment behavior.  
* Quickly populate the terrain generated by the Demo Orchestrator.

It is not meant for:

* Scattering onto arbitrary mesh surfaces.  
* Local brush painting.  
* Large production scatter management.  
* Advanced biome rules.  
* Automatic overlap avoidance.  
* Runtime placement.  
* Texture atlas or material combining.  
* Full environment optimization.

Lite gives you a useful basic placer. Pro is the better option when you need more control.

---

### **Target Environment**

The **Target Environment** section contains the Terrain field.

Rock Generator Lite’s placer works with Unity Terrain. It samples terrain height, terrain normal, terrain size, and terrain position to decide where each rock instance should be placed.

If the Terrain field is empty, use:

**Auto-Find Terrain in Scene**

This searches for an available Terrain object in the open scene and assigns it if found.

The Lite placer does not scatter onto arbitrary meshes. Use a Unity Terrain as the target surface.

---

### **Terrain-Based Placement Only**

The Lite Rock Placer is terrain-based.

It does not place rocks on:

* Mesh cliffs.  
* Modular meshes.  
* Imported landscapes.  
* Building geometry.  
* Custom mesh caves.  
* Other prefab surfaces.

If your environment is built from mesh pieces instead of Unity Terrain, place rocks manually or use another placement workflow.

Rock Generator Pro is the better choice when you need a more production-oriented placement toolset.

---

### **Prefab Palette**

The **Prefab Palette** is the list of rock prefabs that may be scattered.

Each entry has:

**Prefab**  
 The prefab that may be placed.

**Weight**  
 The relative chance that this prefab is selected when a rock is placed.

A higher weight makes a prefab appear more often. A lower weight makes it appear less often.

For example:

* Weight 5 appears much more often than weight 1\.  
* Weight 1 still appears, but less frequently.  
* To remove a prefab from the palette, use the X button for that entry.

The UI weight slider uses positive values. If you do not want a prefab included, remove the palette entry rather than trying to set it to zero.

---

### **Using Multiple Rock Variants**

The Rock Placer works best when the palette contains several related variants.

A good Lite workflow is:

1. Generate a small set of rocks from the same preset or material style.  
2. Use different seeds for silhouette variation.  
3. Keep materials reasonably consistent.  
4. Add those prefabs to the palette.  
5. Use weights to make common rocks appear more often than distinctive rocks.  
6. Use Scale Range to reduce repetition.

For example, a canyon scatter set might include:

* A common sandstone boulder.  
* A flatter sedimentary slab.  
* A taller broken pillar.  
* A darker weathered variant.  
* A smaller debris-like variant.

This gives the terrain more variety without requiring every placed rock to be a unique baked prefab.

---

### **Adding Prefabs from the Demo Orchestrator**

When you bake a demo rock in the Demo Orchestrator and use **Push to Rock Placer Window**, the generated prefab is added to the Rock Placer palette.

The Demo Orchestrator also tries to assign the current demo terrain as the target Terrain.

This handoff does not modify the prefab asset. It only opens the Rock Placer and adds the generated prefab to the palette.

The generated prefab remains saved in the demo output folder.

---

### **Adding Prefabs from the Rock Window**

Opening the Rock Placer from the Rock Window does not automatically send the current preview rock to the placer.

The preview rock is temporary and unsaved.

To use a rock in the placer:

1. Generate the rock prefab from the Rock Window.  
2. Open the Rock Placer.  
3. Add the generated prefab to the Prefab Palette.

Only saved prefabs can be scattered.

---

### **Placement Settings**

The **Placement Settings** section controls the target number of rocks and the random scale range.

---

### **Spawn Count**

**Spawn Count** is the target number of rocks the placer attempts to create.

The actual number placed may be lower than the target count.

This can happen when:

* Slope limits reject too many positions.  
* Height limits reject too many positions.  
* Clump Threshold is too restrictive.  
* The terrain does not contain enough valid placement area.  
* Spawn Count is high and the tool reaches its attempt limit.

The placer tries a limited number of random candidate positions. If too many candidates are rejected, it stops with fewer placed objects.

If fewer rocks appear than expected, loosen the rules before increasing Spawn Count.

---

### **Scale Range**

**Scale Range** applies a random uniform scale to each spawned instance.

The final scale is based on the prefab’s existing scale multiplied by the random value from this range.

This matters if the prefab itself already uses Prefab Scale or has been scaled in the project.

For example:

* A prefab with normal scale and a placer scale of 1.5 appears 1.5× larger.  
* A prefab that already has a larger internal Prefab Scale will be larger before the placer scale is applied.  
* A wide scale range can create useful variation, but it can also make the same rock look inconsistent.

Use moderate scale ranges for natural scatter.

For example:

* 0.8–1.3 for subtle variation.  
* 0.5–2.0 for more obvious variation.  
* Very wide ranges only when the rock set is meant to represent different sizes of the same material.

For large size differences, it is often better to generate separate small, medium, and large variants rather than relying entirely on one prefab scaled heavily.

---

### **Constraints and Rules**

The **Constraints & Rules** section controls where rocks are allowed to spawn.

The main filters are:

* Slope Limits.  
* Height Limits.  
* Clump rule.

Each candidate position must pass these filters before a rock is placed.

If nothing appears, this section is usually where the problem is.

---

### **Slope Limits**

**Slope Limits** restrict placement based on terrain slope angle.

The placer samples the terrain normal and calculates its angle from world up.

Use slope limits to control where rocks appear:

* Low slope ranges place rocks on flatter ground.  
* Mid slope ranges place rocks on hillsides.  
* High slope ranges place rocks on steep terrain.

Examples:

**0–25 degrees**  
 Useful for flatter ground scatter, paths, riverbeds, and general boulders.

**20–55 degrees**  
 Useful for hillsides, mountain slopes, and eroded terrain.

**45–80 degrees**  
 Useful for cliff faces or steep rocky areas, though surface alignment becomes more important.

If the terrain is mostly flat and your minimum slope is high, few or no rocks will spawn.

If the terrain is steep and your maximum slope is low, steep areas will be rejected.

---

### **Height Limits**

**Height Limits** restrict placement based on world-space height.

This is useful for limiting rocks to elevation bands.

Use height limits for:

* Lowland river stones.  
* Mid-height hillside boulders.  
* High alpine rocks.  
* Clifftop scatter.  
* Shoreline rocks.  
* Volcanic vent or mountain-only scatter.

Height is checked in world space, including the Terrain object’s position.

If your Terrain is moved upward or downward in the scene, adjust Height Limits accordingly.

If nothing spawns, widen the height range first to confirm that height filtering is not blocking placement.

---

### **Align to Surface**

**Align to Surface** controls whether each spawned rock rotates toward the sampled terrain normal.

When disabled, rocks receive random yaw but remain upright.

When enabled, rocks blend toward the terrain slope.

This is useful for seating rocks onto hillsides, cliffs, and uneven terrain.

For flat ground, alignment may not make much visible difference.

For steep terrain, alignment can help rocks feel attached to the surface instead of floating or cutting through the slope.

---

### **Alignment Blend**

**Alignment Blend** controls how strongly the rock aligns to the terrain normal.

**0** keeps the rock upright.  
 **1** aligns the rock fully to the surface normal.

Values between 0 and 1 usually look more natural.

A fully aligned rock on a steep slope may look like it is glued flat to the terrain. A partially aligned rock often feels better because real boulders tend to settle into terrain, not perfectly match every slope angle.

Recommended starting values:

* 0.0 for upright pillars or vertical forms.  
* 0.4–0.7 for general boulders.  
* 0.7–0.9 for rocks that should strongly follow hillsides.  
* 1.0 only when full surface alignment is desired.

Tall pillars, spires, and columnar rocks often look better with less alignment. Flat slabs and loose scatter stones usually tolerate more alignment.

---

### **Vertical Sink Offset**

**Vertical Sink Offset** moves each placed rock up or down after placement.

A small negative value helps seat rocks into the terrain so they do not appear to float.

The default negative offset is useful because many rocks have rounded or irregular bottoms. Slightly sinking them into the terrain hides small gaps.

Use:

* Slight negative values for most scatter.  
* Stronger negative values for large boulders that should feel embedded.  
* Positive values only when the prefab visually sits too low.

If Vertical Sink Offset is too negative, rocks may disappear into the terrain or lose too much of their shape.

If it is too positive, rocks may float.

---

### **Clumping**

The Lite Rock Placer can group rocks using a simple Perlin-noise clumping rule.

Clumping does not create intelligent clusters, geological debris fields, or erosion-driven placement. It is a lightweight filter that rejects some random candidate positions based on noise.

It is useful for making scatter less evenly random.

---

### **Clump Noise Scale**

**Clump Noise Scale** controls the size of the clump pattern.

Lower values create broader placement regions. Rocks appear in larger, more open clusters.

Higher values create tighter and more frequent variation. Rocks may appear in smaller, more scattered patches.

Use low-to-mid values for natural terrain scatter.

Very high values can make the distribution feel noisy rather than clustered.

---

### **Clump Threshold**

**Clump Threshold** controls how selective the clump rule is.

Higher values reject more candidate points, creating sparser placement.

Lower values allow more rocks through.

Set Clump Threshold to **0** to effectively disable clump rejection.

If too few rocks appear, reduce Clump Threshold.

If rocks are too evenly spread, increase Clump Threshold slightly.

Avoid pushing the threshold too high unless you want very sparse groups.

---

### **Populating the Terrain**

Use **Populate Terrain** after assigning:

1. A valid Terrain.  
2. At least one valid prefab in the palette.  
3. Positive prefab weights.

The placer creates scene instances of the selected prefabs.

It also creates a parent object named with a **RockScatter** prefix and places the generated instances under that parent. The parent receives a **RockScatterGroup** component so the tool can find and clear generated scatter later.

The scatter root is parented under the target Terrain for organization.

The generated scatter is scene content, not a new prefab asset. Save the scene if you want to keep the placed objects.

---

### **Random Rotation**

Each placed rock receives a random yaw rotation.

If Align to Surface is enabled, the random yaw is combined with the surface alignment.

This helps reduce repetition when the same prefab appears many times.

If a rock has a very directional shape, such as a long slab or column fragment, random yaw can make placement look more varied but may also create some awkward orientations. Use a few different prefab variants to make repetition less obvious.

---

### **Scatter Does Not Check Overlap**

The Lite Rock Placer does not perform advanced overlap or collision avoidance.

Rocks may intersect each other, especially when:

* Spawn Count is high.  
* Scale Range is wide.  
* Clump Threshold creates dense clusters.  
* Large prefabs are used on small terrain areas.  
* Slope and height filters restrict placement to a small region.

If rocks overlap too much:

* Reduce Spawn Count.  
* Reduce Scale Range.  
* Lower Clump Threshold or change Clump Noise Scale.  
* Use smaller prefabs.  
* Scatter in multiple passes with different settings.  
* Manually adjust important rocks after placement.

For production placement with more control, use Rock Generator Pro.

---

### **Clearing Generated Scatter**

Use:

**Clear Generated Scatter (Undo)**

This removes generated scatter groups from the scene.

The Lite placer searches for objects marked with **RockScatterGroup** and removes those scene objects using Unity Undo.

This means the clear operation can be undone through Unity’s normal Undo system.

This clear operation does not delete generated rock prefab assets from the Project window. It only removes generated scatter instances from the scene.

Clearing thousands of objects may briefly slow the editor.

---

### **What Clear Generated Scatter Removes**

Clear Generated Scatter removes scene objects marked as RockScatter groups.

It does not remove:

* Source rock prefabs.  
* Generated rock mesh assets.  
* Baked textures.  
* Materials.  
* Demo output folders.  
* Terrain assets.  
* Manually placed rocks that are not under a RockScatterGroup.

If you manually move rocks out of the scatter group or remove the RockScatterGroup marker, the clear operation may no longer find them.

Use the Project window or Demo Orchestrator purge tools to clean up generated project assets. Use Rock Placer clear only for scene scatter.

---

### **Recommended Lite Placement Workflow**

For a clean Lite scatter pass:

1. Generate a small set of related rock prefabs.  
2. Add them to the Prefab Palette.  
3. Assign weights so common variants appear more often.  
4. Keep Scale Range moderate.  
5. Start with wide slope and height limits.  
6. Populate the terrain.  
7. Check the result.  
8. Tighten slope, height, alignment, and clumping rules.  
9. Clear and repopulate until the distribution feels useful.  
10. Manually adjust important rocks afterward.

Do not start with restrictive settings. First confirm that rocks can spawn across the terrain, then narrow the rules.

---

### **Example Placement Setups**

#### **General Boulder Scatter**

Use this for common terrain dressing.

Recommended starting point:

* Spawn Count: moderate.  
* Scale Range: 0.8–1.5.  
* Slope Limits: 0–45 degrees.  
* Height Limits: broad.  
* Align to Surface: enabled.  
* Alignment Blend: around 0.6–0.8.  
* Vertical Sink Offset: slightly negative.  
* Clump Threshold: low to moderate.

This works well for granite, mossy boulders, river-worn stones, limestone blocks, and general terrain scatter.

---

#### **Cliff and Hillside Rocks**

Use this for steeper terrain.

Recommended starting point:

* Slope Limits: mid to high.  
* Align to Surface: enabled.  
* Alignment Blend: around 0.7–0.9.  
* Vertical Sink Offset: negative enough to seat rocks into the slope.  
* Scale Range: moderate.  
* Clump Threshold: moderate.

Use flatter or slab-like rocks carefully on steep slopes. Some will look natural, while others may appear pasted onto the surface.

---

#### **Riverbed or Lowland Stones**

Use this for flatter low-elevation scatter.

Recommended starting point:

* Slope Limits: low.  
* Height Limits: narrow around the riverbed or lowland height.  
* Scale Range: smaller.  
* Align to Surface: enabled or disabled depending on terrain.  
* Vertical Sink Offset: slightly negative.  
* Clump Threshold: low.

Rounded Icosphere-based rocks usually work well here.

---

#### **Desert or Canyon Scatter**

Use this for sandstone, shale, and canyon-like environments.

Recommended starting point:

* Use several sedimentary presets.  
* Include slabs and boulders.  
* Use scale variation carefully.  
* Use Slope Limits to separate flat ledges from steeper cliff debris.  
* Use Clumping to avoid perfectly even distribution.

Terraced and layered rocks can look strong in canyon scenes, but too many identical slab shapes will repeat visibly. Generate several seed variations.

---

### **Working With Prefab Scale and Placer Scale**

Rock Generator Lite already has two rock scale concepts:

**Target Diameter**  
 The generated procedural mesh size.

**Prefab Scale**  
 The final scale applied to the generated prefab/LOD children.

The Rock Placer adds another scale layer:

**Instance Scale**  
 The random scale applied when the prefab is placed in the scene.

For simple scatter variation, use the Rock Placer Scale Range.

For a rock that should be fundamentally authored as a different size, adjust the generator settings and create a separate prefab.

A practical workflow is:

1. Keep presets around their intended Target Diameter, often 2 meters.  
2. Use Prefab Scale for authored prefab size.  
3. Use Rock Placer Scale Range for natural scene variation.

Avoid scaling one small rock prefab extremely large across a scene. The shape and texture detail may start to look wrong for the apparent size.

---

### **Performance Considerations**

The Lite Rock Placer creates normal prefab instances in the scene.

Large scatter counts can increase:

* Scene object count.  
* Renderer count.  
* Collider count.  
* Draw calls.  
* Editor selection and hierarchy overhead.  
* Scene save size.

This is especially noticeable if every generated rock uses a unique baked material and texture set.

For better Lite performance:

* Reuse a smaller set of rock prefabs.  
* Avoid unnecessary colliders on background scatter.  
* Use lower LOD densities for small or distant rocks.  
* Keep material variation under control.  
* Use Vertex Color mode only when its lower visual fidelity is acceptable.  
* Avoid very high Spawn Count values unless needed.

Rock Generator Pro includes a material and texture atlas combiner for rocks and other assets. That can help reduce material fragmentation and improve batching opportunities depending on render pipeline and scene setup.

---

### **Lite Placer Limits**

The Lite Rock Placer is intentionally basic.

Main limits:

* It scatters onto Unity Terrain only.  
* It does not scatter onto arbitrary meshes.  
* It does not include local brush placement.  
* It does not include advanced placement rules.  
* It does not perform overlap avoidance.  
* It does not batch-generate rock sets.  
* It does not combine materials or textures.  
* It creates scene instances, not a new optimized scatter asset.

These limits are acceptable for testing, small scenes, and simple scatter passes.

They become more noticeable in production environments.

---

### **When to Use Rock Generator Pro for Placement**

Use Rock Generator Pro when the Lite placer starts to feel like a bottleneck.

Pro is the recommended version if you need:

* A more capable Rock Placer.  
* Local brush placement.  
* Larger placement workflows.  
* Batch rock generation.  
* More procedural variation.  
* Better organization for larger rock sets.  
* Higher texture bake resolutions.  
* More texturing options.  
* More texture inputs.  
* Material and texture atlas combining.

Lite is enough to test the generated rocks and make simple terrain scatter. Pro is the better fit when placement becomes part of the actual environment production workflow.

### **Rock Placer Troubleshooting**

**Nothing appears when I click Populate Terrain.**  
 Check that a Terrain is assigned, the prefab palette contains valid prefabs, and the placement rules are not too restrictive.

**Only a few rocks appear.**  
 Widen slope limits, widen height limits, lower Clump Threshold, or reduce Spawn Count and test again.

**Rocks are floating.**  
 Use a slightly more negative Vertical Sink Offset.

**Rocks are buried too deeply.**  
 Raise Vertical Sink Offset toward zero.

**Rocks look pasted flat onto steep slopes.**  
 Lower Alignment Blend.

**Tall pillars are leaning too much.**  
 Disable Align to Surface or reduce Alignment Blend.

**The scatter looks too random and even.**  
 Increase Clump Threshold slightly or lower Clump Noise Scale for broader clusters.

**The scatter is too sparse.**  
 Lower Clump Threshold or widen the slope/height rules.

**Rocks overlap too much.**  
 Reduce Spawn Count, narrow Scale Range, use smaller prefabs, or manually adjust the important placements.

**Clear Generated Scatter did not remove some rocks.**  
 Only objects under scatter groups marked with RockScatterGroup are cleared. Manually moved or unmarked objects may need to be deleted manually.

---

### **Summary**

The Lite Rock Placer is a practical scatter tool for Unity Terrain. It is best used with a small palette of generated rock prefabs, moderate scale variation, and simple slope/height/clump rules.

Use it to test rocks quickly, populate demo terrain, and create simple scatter layouts.

For production placement, local brush control, larger rock sets, stronger placement workflows, and better material organization, Rock Generator Pro is the recommended upgrade.

## **9\. Editor Scripting and Limited Runtime Use**

Rock Generator Lite is primarily an **Editor generation tool**.

The intended workflow is:

1. Create or edit a RockSettings profile.  
2. Preview the rock in the Rock Window.  
3. Generate a prefab in the Editor.  
4. Place or scatter the saved prefab in your scene.  
5. Use the saved prefab in builds like a normal Unity asset.

Lite does include runtime-side mesh generation infrastructure, because the Editor tools use that same generation pipeline internally. However, Lite should not be treated as a full runtime rock-generation product.

For most projects, use Lite to generate clean prefabs in the Editor. Use Rock Generator Pro if runtime generation, runtime MonoBehaviour tools, batch generation, or larger procedural workflows are important to your project.

---

### **Main Data Type: RockSettings**

**RockSettings** is the main profile asset used by Rock Generator Lite.

It is a ScriptableObject that stores the procedural recipe for a rock, including:

* Output folder.  
* Export name.  
* Base mesh shape.  
* Rock type.  
* Seed.  
* Target diameter.  
* Prefab scale.  
* Proportions.  
* Macro and detail noise.  
* Domain warping.  
* Voronoi.  
* Terracing.  
* Procedural texturing.  
* Bump and micro-detail settings.  
* Texture export settings.  
* LOD settings.  
* Collider settings.

You can create a RockSettings asset from the Unity asset menu:

**Procedural Generation \> New Rock Settings**

Saved RockSettings profiles are useful when you want to reuse a procedural setup, create multiple seed variations, or keep a small library of rock styles.

A RockSettings profile is not a finished rock mesh. It is the recipe used to generate a rock.

---

### **Opening a Profile in the Rock Window**

Saved RockSettings assets can be opened in the Rock Window.

When a persistent RockSettings asset is selected in the Project window, its custom inspector includes:

**Open in Live Preview Window**

This opens the Rock Window with that profile assigned as the base asset.

The Rock Window edits a temporary copy first. Changes made in the Rock Window are not automatically written back to the source RockSettings asset.

Use:

**Apply to '\[Profile Name\]'**  
 Writes the current preview settings back to the assigned profile.

**Save As...**  
 Creates a new RockSettings asset from the current edited settings.

This clone-first behavior is useful. It lets you test seeds, presets, colors, and noise settings without immediately damaging a saved profile.

---

### **Editing RockSettings from Editor Code**

Advanced users can create or modify RockSettings assets from custom Editor tools.

This can be useful for:

* Small custom profile libraries.  
* Studio-specific naming conventions.  
* Automated setup of common output folders.  
* Simple internal tools that prepare RockSettings before generation.  
* Creating preset variations from your own Editor scripts.

Use caution when editing persistent RockSettings assets from code. Applying presets or changing fields directly can overwrite large parts of a profile.

A safe Editor workflow is:

1. Load or create a RockSettings asset.  
2. Work on a temporary copy.  
3. Apply changes intentionally.  
4. Save the modified copy as a new asset when branching styles.

This mirrors how the Rock Window works internally.

---

### **Editor Prefab Generation**

The Editor prefab generation pipeline is handled by **RockPrefabFactory**.

The main public method is:

`RockPrefabFactory.CreateAndSavePrefab(settings, onComplete, showProgressBar)`

This method is Editor-only.

It performs the full generated-prefab workflow:

1. Creates an organized output folder.  
2. Starts the hidden Editor generation service.  
3. Generates the procedural LOD meshes.  
4. Bakes textures when using a baked texture output mode.  
5. Saves texture files to disk when needed.  
6. Imports generated textures.  
7. Creates a material.  
8. Saves generated mesh assets.  
9. Creates the prefab.  
10. Assigns LODs, materials, textures, and colliders.  
11. Reports the generated prefab through the completion callback.

This is useful if you want to build a small custom Editor tool around Lite.

For large batch workflows, use Rock Generator Pro instead. Pro is designed for broader generation and asset-management workflows, while Lite is intended for individual rocks and smaller sets.

---

### **Editor Generation Is Callback-Based**

Prefab generation is not just an immediate synchronous return value.

The generation pipeline uses jobs, texture baking, asset writing, and AssetDatabase import work. The completion callback receives the generated prefab when the process finishes.

A practical Editor scripting pattern is:

1. Check that the RockSettings profile is valid.  
2. Call `RockPrefabFactory.CreateAndSavePrefab`.  
3. Use the callback to receive the generated prefab.  
4. Do any follow-up work after the callback runs.

Avoid assuming that the prefab exists immediately after calling the method.

Also avoid starting multiple generations at the same time. The factory tracks active generation and will reject a new request if another generation is already running.

---

### **Presets from Editor Code**

The Lite presets are applied through **RockPresetUtility**.

The main method is:

`RockPresetUtility.ApplyPreset(settings, preset)`

This method is Editor-only.

Applying a preset changes many fields on the target RockSettings object. It is not a small cosmetic change.

A preset may change:

* Export name.  
* Base shape.  
* Proportions.  
* Rock type behavior.  
* Macro noise.  
* Detail noise.  
* Domain warping.  
* Voronoi.  
* Terracing.  
* Color pattern.  
* Main colors.  
* Cavity color.  
* Bump settings.  
* Smoothness.  
* Metallic style.  
* LOD transitions.

Use a copy if you do not want to overwrite the original profile.

A safe pattern is:

1. Create a new RockSettings instance.  
2. Apply a preset.  
3. Randomize the seed if desired.  
4. Set the output folder and export name.  
5. Generate the prefab.  
6. Save the profile only if you want to keep it.

---

### **Quick Start Presets Are Editor Profiles, Not Rock Types**

Quick Start Presets are separate from the **Rock Type** setting.

Rock Type is a broad procedural shortcut:

* Igneous.  
* Metamorphic.  
* Sedimentary.  
* Custom.

Quick Start Presets are authored profile setups such as sandstone, obsidian, basalt, granite, shale, pumice, quartz, marble, and mineral-bearing styles.

If you are scripting around presets, treat them as full profile setups. Do not treat them as only a label or category.

---

### **Rock Placer Handoff**

The Demo Orchestrator hands generated prefabs to the Rock Placer through Lite Editor tooling.

The Rock Placer can receive:

* A target Terrain.  
* A list of prefab references.

This is mainly used by the Demo Orchestrator, but it can also be useful as a reference point for custom Editor workflows.

The Lite Rock Placer remains a basic Terrain scatter tool. It is not a general placement framework, and it does not scatter onto arbitrary mesh surfaces.

Use Rock Generator Pro when you need more advanced placement workflows, local brush placement, or larger production placement tools.

---

### **Package Assembly Structure**

Rock Generator Lite is split into four assemblies:

**Main Runtime Assembly**  
 `Veridian.RockGenLite.Runtime`

**Main Editor Assembly**  
 `Veridian.RockGenLite.Editor`

**Demo Runtime Assembly**  
 `Veridian.RockGenLite.Demo.Runtime`

**Demo Editor Assembly**  
 `Veridian.RockGenLite.Demo.Editor`

The Editor assemblies are placed in Editor folders and are excluded from player builds.

The runtime assemblies remain separate from the Editor tools. This means you should not describe the package as having no runtime code at all. Lite includes runtime-side support classes such as the lower-level runtime generator and scatter marker components.

The more precise statement is:

**The Editor tools are excluded from builds, and generated baked prefabs do not require the Rock Generator Lite Editor windows to function after export.**

---

### **Generated Baked Prefabs Do Not Need Generator MonoBehaviours**

A generated baked rock prefab is normal Unity project content.

It can contain:

* A root GameObject.  
* Child LOD objects.  
* MeshFilter components.  
* MeshRenderer components.  
* A Unity LODGroup.  
* A generated material.  
* Generated texture references.  
* Optional colliders.

Generated baked prefabs do not require Rock Generator Lite Editor MonoBehaviours to function after export.

This is an important part of the Lite workflow. The generator is used to create the asset. The saved prefab is then used like a normal environment prop.

Vertex Color output still requires an appropriate material or shader that displays vertex colors. If the shader does not read vertex colors, the rock may not display correctly.

---

### **Lite and Pro in the Same Project**

Rock Generator Lite and Rock Generator Pro use different namespaces and assemblies, so they can safely exist in the same Unity project.

However, if you own Rock Generator Pro, you should generally remove Lite unless you have a specific reason to keep both installed.

Pro covers the Lite workflow and adds the fuller toolset. Keeping both versions installed can make menus, examples, generated folders, and project organization more cluttered.

Use both only when you intentionally need to compare Lite behavior with Pro behavior or maintain compatibility with old Lite-generated test content.

---

### **Runtime Generation in Lite**

Lite includes **RuntimeRockGenerator** and **RockRequest**.

These provide lower-level runtime mesh generation capability.

A RockRequest includes:

* A RockSettings profile.  
* A target position.  
* A rotation.  
* A scale.  
* A shared material.  
* A collider generation flag.  
* A completion callback.

The runtime generator builds mesh data and creates a generated GameObject when generation completes.

This is an advanced path. It is not the main Lite workflow.

---

### **What Runtime Generation Can Do**

Runtime generation in Lite can generate procedural mesh GameObjects using RockSettings.

It can create:

* A generated root GameObject.  
* LOD child objects.  
* MeshFilter components.  
* MeshRenderer components.  
* An LODGroup.  
* Runtime mesh instances.  
* Optional colliders depending on the request and settings.

This can be useful for advanced users who want to experiment with runtime procedural mesh creation.

However, runtime generation in Lite is lower-level and less convenient than the Editor workflow. You are responsible for providing a suitable material and managing the generated objects.

---

### **Runtime Texture Limitation**

Runtime texture baking is not part of the Lite workflow.

The Lite texture baker is Editor-only and uses a compute shader through the Editor pipeline.

At runtime, do not expect Lite to bake and save new albedo, normal, mask, AO, height, metallic, or smoothness texture files during gameplay.

If you generate rocks at runtime in Lite, use a suitable shared material. Vertex-color-oriented workflows are generally more practical for this than baked texture workflows, because the runtime path can generate mesh color data but does not perform the full Editor texture bake/export process.

For runtime-oriented projects, Rock Generator Pro is the recommended version because it includes a fuller runtime-oriented setup and runtime MonoBehaviour tools.

---

### **Runtime Material Requirements**

A runtime RockRequest requires a shared material.

If the material is missing, the request cannot generate a usable rendered rock.

The material should match the output style you expect.

For Vertex Color use, the shader must read vertex colors.

For baked-looking use, remember that Lite runtime generation does not bake the texture set at runtime. You would need to use a shared material or your own shader/material workflow.

Do not expect runtime-generated Lite rocks to automatically produce the same material richness as Editor-baked prefab output.

---

### **Runtime Colliders**

Runtime generation can create colliders if the RockRequest asks for colliders and the RockSettings collider type is not None.

The same general collider guidance applies:

* Primitive colliders are cheapest.  
* Convex Mesh is the safer general-purpose option.  
* Exact Mesh is non-convex and should be reserved for static detailed collision.

For runtime generation, be especially careful with colliders. Generating many mesh colliders during gameplay can create performance spikes and memory pressure.

For most projects, generate and save prefabs in the Editor instead.

---

### **Runtime Generated Objects Are Not Saved Assets**

Runtime-generated rocks are scene/runtime objects.

They are not automatically saved as project assets, mesh assets, texture files, or prefabs.

The Editor prefab generation pipeline is what saves generated meshes, materials, textures, and prefabs into the Unity project.

If you need saved reusable assets, use the Rock Window, Demo Orchestrator, or your own Editor tool calling RockPrefabFactory.

---

### **Recommended Runtime Positioning**

For most users, use Lite this way:

1. Generate rocks in the Editor.  
2. Save clean prefabs.  
3. Place the prefabs by hand or with the Rock Placer.  
4. Use those prefabs in builds.

Treat Lite runtime generation as an advanced option, not the default workflow.

Use Rock Generator Pro if runtime generation is a meaningful part of your project. Pro is the better fit for runtime-oriented workflows, runtime MonoBehaviour tools, broader procedural use, and larger production systems.

---

### **When to Write Custom Editor Tools Around Lite**

Custom Editor scripting around Lite can make sense when you need small workflow helpers.

Reasonable Lite editor-tool examples:

* Generate a few named variants from one profile.  
* Apply a preset and save a profile copy.  
* Enforce an output folder convention.  
* Create a studio-specific “generate one rock” button.  
* Send generated prefabs into a simple scene setup.

Less suitable Lite editor-tool examples:

* Generating hundreds of variants.  
* Managing a large production rock library.  
* Building a material optimization pipeline.  
* Creating a full placement workflow.  
* Runtime generation systems.  
* Texture atlas or material combining workflows.

Those heavier cases are better handled by Rock Generator Pro.

---

### **Scripting Summary**

Rock Generator Lite exposes enough scripting surface for advanced Editor users to build small helpers around the generator.

The most important scripting points are:

* RockSettings stores the procedural profile.  
* RockPreviewWindow is the main Editor preview tool.  
* RockPrefabFactory creates and saves prefabs in the Editor.  
* RockPresetUtility applies Lite presets in the Editor.  
* RuntimeRockGenerator can generate runtime mesh objects, but it is a limited advanced path.  
* Runtime texture baking is not part of Lite.  
* Generated baked prefabs are normal Unity assets and do not require the Editor tools after export.

Use Lite scripting for small Editor extensions and experiments. Use Rock Generator Pro when the workflow becomes a larger production system.

## **10\. Performance Limits, Troubleshooting, and Pro Upgrade Notes**

Rock Generator Lite is usable for creating and testing generated rocks, but it has deliberate limits.

Understanding those limits will make the tool easier to use and will help you decide when Rock Generator Pro becomes the better fit.

Lite is strongest when used as an Editor generator for individual rocks or small reusable sets.

The recommended workflow is:

1. Generate rocks in the Editor.  
2. Save them as prefabs.  
3. Reuse those prefabs in scenes.  
4. Scatter them with the Rock Placer when useful.  
5. Keep material and texture counts under control.

---

### **Lite Is Best Used as an Editor Generator**

Rock Generator Lite should usually be used before runtime, not during runtime.

Use Lite to create the rock assets, then use those assets normally in your scene or build.

This keeps runtime behavior more predictable because the heavy work has already been done:

* Mesh generation.  
* Texture baking.  
* Texture file creation.  
* Asset importing.  
* Material creation.  
* Prefab saving.  
* Collider setup.

Runtime generation exists at a lower level, but it is not the main Lite workflow. If runtime generation is central to your project, use Rock Generator Pro.

---

### **Texture Resolution Limit**

Rock Generator Lite caps texture baking at:

**1024×1024**

The interface may show 2048 and 4096 options as Pro features, but those resolutions are blocked or clamped in Lite.

Use Rock Generator Pro when you need:

* 2K rock textures.  
* 4K rock textures.  
* Higher-resolution hero props.  
* Larger environment production.  
* More serious material authoring.

Lite’s 1024 cap is enough for many small rocks, background boulders, test assets, and moderate scene props. It is less suitable for close-up hero rocks, cinematic assets, or high-resolution material libraries.

---

### **Compute Shader Requirement**

Procedural texture baking uses:

**RockBakerLite.compute**

If the compute shader is missing or the target platform does not support Compute Shaders, baked texture generation will fail.

Check the following if baking does not work:

* The project supports Compute Shaders.  
* The active graphics API supports the compute workflow.  
* `RockBakerLite.compute` exists in the project.  
* The selected texture resolution is within Lite’s supported range.  
* The GPU and driver are suitable for the bake.  
* The project is not missing required render pipeline shader support.

Mesh generation and texture baking are separate systems. A rock may be able to generate mesh data while baked texture output still fails because the compute shader path is unavailable.

---

### **Texture Baking Can Pause the Editor**

Texture baking uses GPU compute work, reads texture data back to the CPU, writes image files to disk, and asks Unity to import those generated files.

Some Editor delay is normal, especially when using:

* 1024 resolution.  
* Full-resolution normal maps.  
* Full-resolution auxiliary maps.  
* AO generation.  
* Height map generation.  
* Smoothness map generation.  
* Metallic/mineral styles.  
* Multiple generated maps.  
* Several rocks generated one after another.

Use lower preview quality while designing shape and color. Switch to 1024 only when you need a closer look at the material.

Use Pro for higher-resolution and more production-focused workflows.

---

### **Unique Materials and Draw Calls**

When Lite bakes a rock, it can create a unique material and texture set for that generated rock.

This is useful for variation, but it can become inefficient if every rock in a large scene has its own material and texture set.

A practical Lite workflow is:

1. Generate a small set of reusable rock prefabs.  
2. Reuse those prefabs throughout the scene.  
3. Use Prefab Scale and Rock Placer instance scale for variation.  
4. Avoid generating a unique baked material for every placed rock.  
5. Disable unnecessary colliders on background scatter.

Rock Generator Pro includes a material and texture atlas combiner for rocks and other assets. Depending on your render pipeline and scene setup, atlas combining can reduce material fragmentation and improve batching opportunities.

If you are manually managing many generated materials in Lite, that is a strong sign that Pro is the better workflow.

---

### **Mesh Density and LOD Cost**

Higher mesh density gives the procedural displacement more geometry to work with. It can improve silhouettes and make geometry noise read better.

It also increases:

* Mesh memory.  
* Generation time.  
* Asset size.  
* Renderer cost.  
* Collider cost if exact mesh collision is used.  
* Scene complexity when many rocks are placed.

Use high-density LOD 0 only when the rock will be seen close to the camera.

For smaller or distant rocks, lower density is usually better.

Use the Rock Window’s LOD preview and triangle count display before generating the prefab.

---

### **LODs Are Not a Fix for Every Heavy Rock**

LODs help reduce render cost at distance, but they do not automatically solve every performance problem.

A scene can still become heavy if it contains:

* Too many unique materials.  
* Too many unique texture sets.  
* Too many high-density LOD 0 meshes near the camera.  
* Too many colliders.  
* Too many separate GameObjects.  
* Too many large scattered groups.  
* Expensive shaders or custom materials.

Use LODs as one part of the workflow, not the entire optimization strategy.

For larger rock sets, Pro’s batch and material-combining tools become more useful.

---

### **Collider Cost**

Collider choice affects runtime performance and physics behavior.

Use the simplest collider that works.

**None**  
 Best for decorative rocks with no gameplay contact.

**Primitive Box**  
 Cheap and useful for rough blocking or simple background collision.

**Primitive Sphere**  
 Cheap and useful for rounded boulders or small stones.

**Convex Mesh**  
 Good general-purpose choice for most rocks that need more accurate collision.

**Exact Mesh**  
 Non-convex and more expensive. Use only for static detailed collision such as large cliffs, walkable shelves, or special environment pieces.

Do not use Exact Mesh as the default collider for scattered rocks.

If physics performance becomes poor, switch Exact Mesh colliders to Convex Mesh or primitives, and remove colliders from purely decorative scatter.

---

### **Rock Placer Limits**

The Lite Rock Placer is intentionally basic.

It scatters prefabs on Unity Terrain using:

* Prefab palette weights.  
* Spawn count.  
* Scale range.  
* Slope limits.  
* Height limits.  
* Surface alignment.  
* Vertical offset.  
* Perlin-noise clumping.

It does not scatter onto arbitrary mesh surfaces. It does not include local brush placement. It does not perform advanced overlap avoidance. It does not create a fully optimized scatter asset.

This is enough for testing, demos, simple scenes, and small terrain scatter passes.

Use Rock Generator Pro when placement becomes part of your actual production workflow.

---

### **Generated Assets Are Saved to Disk**

Generating a prefab creates project assets.

Depending on settings, a generated rock folder may contain:

* Prefab.  
* Mesh assets.  
* Collider mesh asset.  
* Material.  
* Albedo texture.  
* Normal texture.  
* Packed mask map.  
* Metallic map.  
* AO map.  
* Height map.  
* Smoothness map.

Keep generated content organized.

Use separate folders for:

* Demo output.  
* Temporary tests.  
* Production rock sets.  
* Biomes.  
* Level-specific rocks.  
* Material families.

The **Undo Last Generation** button deletes the most recently generated rock folder from disk. It is useful for cleanup, but it is not Unity scene Undo.

Use it carefully.

---

### **Generated Output Ownership Reminder**

Generated meshes, materials, textures, and prefabs are generated output assets.

You may use, modify, sell, and redistribute those generated output assets in your own projects, games, scenes, demos, rendered media, asset packs, or commercial work.

This permission applies to generated output assets only.

It does not allow redistribution of:

* Rock Generator Lite package files.  
* Rock Generator Pro package files.  
* Source code.  
* Editor tools.  
* Compute shaders.  
* Documentation.  
* Demo scripts.  
* Original package files.

A generated rock prefab is your output asset. The generator itself is still the asset package.

---

### **Common Issue: Generated Textures Are Black, Pink, or Missing**

Check the following:

* The target platform supports Compute Shaders.  
* `RockBakerLite.compute` exists in the project.  
* The generated material uses a shader compatible with your render pipeline.  
* The albedo and normal textures were generated and imported.  
* The selected output folder is inside Assets.  
* The Console does not show a compute shader or AssetDatabase error.  
* The project has URP Lit, HDRP Lit, or Built-in Standard shader support available, depending on your render pipeline.

Pink materials usually indicate a shader problem. Missing or black textures usually indicate a bake, import, path, or material assignment issue.

If you use a custom shader, you may need to manually assign the generated textures to the correct shader properties.

---

### **Common Issue: The Rock Looks Uncolored in Vertex Color Mode**

Vertex Color mode requires a shader or material that reads vertex colors.

If the shader ignores vertex color data, the mesh may appear white, gray, plain, or incorrectly colored.

Vertex Color mode is best used for lightweight/mobile-style output or custom shaders. It is not the best visual target for most included presets.

For best visual quality, use Procedural Texture Bake.

---

### **Common Issue: The Rock Looks Too Shiny**

Lower **Base Smoothness**.

Most dry rock should use low smoothness.

Higher smoothness is useful for specific materials, such as:

* Wet stone.  
* Obsidian.  
* Polished marble.  
* Quartz.  
* Metallic deposits.  
* Stylized fantasy minerals.

If a normal dry boulder looks like plastic, wet glass, or polished resin, smoothness is usually too high.

Also check metallic settings. High metallic and high smoothness together can dominate the material quickly.

---

### **Common Issue: The Rock Looks Noisy or Glittery**

This usually comes from overusing normal detail.

Check:

* Bump Strength.  
* Bump Scale.  
* Baked Normal Strength.  
* Micro Detail Scale.  
* Micro Strength.  
* Base Smoothness.  
* Ore Smoothness.

Procedural bump is useful for most baked rocks, but it should usually be moderate.

Micro-detail should be subtle. It can help sandstone, chalk, pumice, mudstone, and rough limestone, but high values can create noisy lighting and specular flicker.

If the silhouette needs more form, use geometry noise. Do not try to solve silhouette problems with micro-detail.

---

### **Common Issue: The Bump Does Not Seem to Change the Shape**

Bump affects the baked normal map.

It changes lighting, not geometry.

Use bump for:

* Surface grain.  
* Fine roughness.  
* Pores.  
* Small scratches.  
* Lighting detail.

Use geometry controls for:

* Silhouette changes.  
* Chips.  
* Ledges.  
* Terraces.  
* Fractures.  
* Large cracks.  
* Boulder mass.

If the rock outline is too smooth, adjust shape and noise settings rather than bump.

---

### **Common Issue: The Height Map Does Not Seem to Do Anything**

Lite can generate a separate height texture when height generation is enabled.

The height texture only has a visible effect if your material or shader uses it.

If your shader does not support height, parallax, or displacement-style effects, generating the height map will not visibly change the material.

Also remember that in Lite, height is not packed into the blue channel of the mask map. Height is saved separately when enabled.

---

### **Common Issue: 2K or 4K Baking Is Blocked**

This is expected in Lite.

Rock Generator Lite is capped at 1024×1024 texture baking.

2048 and 4096 are Pro features.

Use Rock Generator Pro if you need 2K or 4K output.

---

### **Common Issue: The Preview Looks Stale**

When using baked texture modes, the Rock Window may wait briefly before refreshing preview textures. This avoids rebaking on every small slider movement.

Use:

**Force Update Colors**

when the material preview does not seem to reflect recent changes to color, bump, AO, metallic, smoothness, or texture settings.

Use:

**Refresh**

if the preview appears corrupted, broken, or out of sync rather than merely stale.

---

### **Common Issue: Generation Does Not Produce a Prefab**

Check the following:

* RockSettings is not null.  
* The profile has at least one LOD level.  
* The output folder is inside Assets.  
* The generator is not already running.  
* The Console does not show a compute shader, material, mesh, or AssetDatabase error.  
* If using baked output, the compute shader exists and works.  
* If using a custom output path, the folder can be created.

The factory prevents overlapping generation. If generation is already active, wait until it finishes before starting another one.

---

### **Common Issue: Undo Last Generation Deleted the Wrong Thing**

Undo Last Generation deletes the most recently generated rock folder recorded by the Rock Window generation pipeline.

It deletes project assets from disk. It is not the same as Unity scene Undo.

Avoid moving unrelated assets into a generated rock folder. If you later use Undo Last Generation, those files may be deleted along with the generated folder.

Use dedicated output folders and keep generated content organized.

---

### **Common Issue: Nothing Appears When Using the Rock Placer**

Check:

* A Terrain is assigned.  
* The prefab palette contains valid prefabs.  
* Prefab weights are greater than zero.  
* Slope limits match the terrain.  
* Height limits match the terrain.  
* Clump Threshold is not too high.  
* Spawn Count is not blocked by restrictive placement rules.

The placer attempts a limited number of random positions. If too many candidate points fail the rules, fewer rocks will be placed.

Start with broad slope and height limits, then narrow them after confirming that placement works.

---

### **Common Issue: Fewer Rocks Spawn Than Requested**

This is usually caused by rejected placement candidates.

Possible causes:

* Slope limits are too narrow.  
* Height limits are too narrow.  
* Clump Threshold is too high.  
* Terrain does not contain enough valid surface area.  
* Spawn Count is high relative to valid placement area.

Reduce restrictions before increasing Spawn Count.

---

### **Common Issue: Rocks Float or Sink Too Deeply**

Adjust **Vertical Sink Offset** in the Rock Placer.

Use a small negative value to seat rocks into the terrain.

If rocks float, make the offset more negative.

If rocks are buried too deeply, move the offset closer to zero.

Large rocks and flat slabs may need different offsets. For important placed rocks, manual adjustment after scattering is normal.

---

### **Common Issue: Rocks Look Repetitive**

Use more variation.

Good Lite options:

* Generate several seed variants from the same preset.  
* Mix several related prefabs in the Rock Placer palette.  
* Use moderate scale variation.  
* Use random yaw from the placer.  
* Adjust weights so one distinctive rock does not appear too often.  
* Use different slab, boulder, and debris shapes for the same biome.

Avoid relying on one baked rock prefab scaled across an entire terrain. Repetition becomes obvious quickly.

For larger variant libraries and batch workflows, use Rock Generator Pro.

---

### **Common Issue: The Scene Becomes Heavy After Scattering**

Check:

* Spawn Count.  
* Number of unique materials.  
* Number of unique texture sets.  
* Collider count.  
* LOD density.  
* Whether background rocks need colliders.  
* Whether every rock is a unique generated prefab.  
* Whether the same material could be reused.

A good Lite workflow is to generate a small reusable set and scatter instances of those prefabs.

If the project needs many unique rocks, material combining, atlas workflows, or larger placement systems, use Rock Generator Pro.

---

### **Common Issue: Exact Mesh Colliders Are Expensive**

Use Exact Mesh only for static detailed collision.

For most rocks, use Convex Mesh or primitive colliders.

Exact Mesh colliders are non-convex and can be too costly for ordinary scatter, dynamic physics objects, or large numbers of repeated props.

If collision performance is poor, switch to:

* Convex Mesh.  
* Primitive Box.  
* Primitive Sphere.  
* No collider for decorative rocks.

---

### **Common Issue: Runtime Generation Does Not Look Like Editor-Baked Output**

This is expected.

Lite runtime generation can create mesh objects, but runtime texture baking is not part of Lite.

Editor-baked prefabs may include generated albedo, normal, mask, AO, height, metallic, and smoothness textures.

Runtime-generated Lite rocks require a shared material and do not automatically create the same baked texture set.

Use Editor-generated prefabs for the best Lite output. Use Rock Generator Pro for runtime-oriented workflows.

---

### **Practical Lite Production Advice**

For small projects or prototypes:

1. Use presets.  
2. Generate several variants.  
3. Keep Target Diameter near the preset scale.  
4. Use Prefab Scale and scene scale for variation.  
5. Use Procedural Texture Bake for best visual quality.  
6. Keep texture resolution appropriate.  
7. Use Convex Mesh or primitive colliders.  
8. Scatter with a small prefab palette.  
9. Reuse materials and prefabs where possible.

For larger projects:

1. Build organized rock sets by biome or level.  
2. Avoid one unique material per placed rock.  
3. Keep background scatter lightweight.  
4. Check LODs before export.  
5. Avoid Exact Mesh colliders except where needed.  
6. Use Pro when manual generation and organization become slow.

Lite can produce usable rocks, but it is not designed to manage a large production library by itself.

## **11\. Preset Reference / Rock Style Guide**

Rock Generator Lite includes Quick Start Presets that give you useful starting points for different rock styles. These presets are not meant to be perfect geological recreations. They are practical game-art profiles built from the Lite shape, noise, color, bump, metallic, and texture-baking systems.

Some presets are reasonably realistic. Others are stylized, exaggerated, fantasy-oriented, or designed to make a material idea read clearly in a game scene.

Use this section as a style guide. It explains what each preset is trying to represent, where it might fit in a scene, how realistic it is, and what to adjust first.

For most presets, the recommended workflow is:

1. Apply the preset.  
2. Press **New Seed** several times.  
3. Keep **Target Diameter** near 2 meters unless you intentionally want to change procedural scale relationships.  
4. Use **Prefab Scale** for simple scene-size variation.  
5. Adjust proportions before noise.  
6. Adjust color before bump.  
7. Use baked texture output for the best visual result.

Most presets are authored for **Procedural Texture Bake**. Vertex Color mode can approximate some presets, but it usually looks simpler and lower-fidelity. Metallic, mineral, glassy, and crystalline presets especially need baked texture output to read correctly.

Rock Generator Pro expands this preset-based workflow with batch generation, more noise methods, more texturing methods, more texture inputs, higher bake resolutions, a stronger placement workflow, and material/texture atlas combining.

---

### **Realism Categories Used in This Section**

Each preset includes a realism note.

**Reasonably realistic**  
 Based on a real rock, mineral, weathering pattern, or natural environment. It may still be simplified for game use.

**Stylized realistic**  
 Based on a real material, but exaggerated or simplified so the form or color reads better in a scene.

**Highly stylized**  
 Inspired by real materials, but pushed strongly for visual clarity, readability, or game-resource use.

**Fantasy / sci-fi**  
 Not intended to be realistic. Useful for alien worlds, resource nodes, magical materials, or stylized environments.

### **1\. Desert Sandstone**

**Type:** Sedimentary rock / desert slab  
 **Realism:** Reasonably realistic, with simplified game-art strata  
 **Best used as:** Slabs, canyon stones, low boulders, ledges, dry terrain scatter

Desert Sandstone is a warm layered sedimentary preset. It uses a flattened CubeSphere form, sedimentary strata coloring, terracing, orange-brown colors, and darker cavity shading.

This preset is meant to suggest sandstone found in deserts, mesas, badlands, canyon walls, arid cliffs, and eroded sedimentary shelves. The banding is color-based rather than texture-splatted, so it should be treated as a readable game-art approximation rather than a full geological material system.

The preset is reasonably natural when used as a slab or low boulder. It becomes more stylized if scaled into very tall or very sharp shapes.

**Useful environments:**

* Canyons  
* Dry riverbeds  
* Badlands  
* Western-style landscapes

### **Preset Workflow Tips**

Presets are most useful when treated as editable starting points.

A good beginner workflow is:

1. Apply a preset.  
2. Press **New Seed** until the shape feels useful.  
3. Adjust **Prefab Scale** before changing Target Diameter.  
4. Change only one group of settings at a time.  
5. Check LODs before generating.  
6. Use Procedural Texture Bake for the best visual result.

A good production workflow is:

1. Pick a preset family for a biome.  
2. Save one or more edited RockSettings profiles.  
3. Generate several seed variations.  
4. Reuse those prefabs across the scene.  
5. Keep materials and colliders under control.  
6. Use Rock Generator Pro when you need batch generation, richer texture workflows, more input textures, higher bake resolutions, placement brush tools, or atlas/material combining.

### **Presets and Vertex Color Mode**

The included presets are primarily designed for baked texture output.

You can switch a preset to **Vertex Colors**, but the result should be treated as an approximation.

Vertex Color mode works best for:

* Simple boulders.  
* Mobile-style assets.  
* Lower-fidelity scatter.  
* Stylized rocks with broad colors.  
* Custom shaders that read vertex colors.

Vertex Color mode is usually not recommended for:

* Gold Veined Quartz.  
* Alien Geode.  
* Geometric Bismuth Cluster.  
* Oxidized Copper Malachite.  
* Pyrite Slate Slab.  
* Hematite Iron Ore Nodule.  
* Lapis Pyrite Boulder.  
* Any preset that depends heavily on metallic, smoothness, bump, micro-detail, AO, or baked normal maps.

For those presets, use Procedural Texture Bake.
